The [companion HTML](../../Interactive%20Guide%20to%20the%20Entitlement%20Grandiosity%20Schema.html) is intended for adults exploring their own experience; Persian is the default and English conveys the same content. Content, bilingual, structural, and local-link reviews passed, along with forty browser acceptance checks covering desktop/mobile layouts, keyboard access, language switching, worksheet privacy/clearing, storage-denied operation, and printing. **Clinical emphasis:** distinguish a rigid claim to exceptional rights or superiority from self-respect, legitimate accommodation, shared decision-making, and proportionate advocacy. Do not equate the schema with a personality disorder, infer another person's motives, assume that shame lies beneath every entitlement response, or use accountability as a reason for humiliation. Keep impact on others visible alongside the person's needs.

## 2 Understanding this schema

### Schema at a glance / طرحواره در یک نگاه

| Required item | Content to preserve in both languages |
| --- | --- |
| Brief description | An enduring expectation of superior status, special rights, or exemption from ordinary reciprocity can make it difficult to consider other people's equally valid needs and limits. This is a conceptual pattern, not a conclusion from one request or disagreement. [1](#ref-1) [2](#ref-2) |
| Domain | **Impaired Limits / محدودیت‌های مختل**; link to [the overview's domain entry](../../Guide%20to%20Schema%20Domains%20and%20Recognition.html#domain-limits). The domain concerns internal limits, reciprocal responsibility, and cooperation; it groups schemas and does not diagnose a person. [2](#ref-2) |
| Relevant needs | Realistic limits and self-control are central to the conceptual formulation. Secure attachment/acceptance and freedom to express valid needs/emotions may also matter in a particular person's experience. Discuss selected needs with examples in section 3, without treating the mapping as a causal finding. [1](#ref-1) [4](#ref-4) |
| Possible central message | **Original illustration:** “My priorities deserve an exception; this limit should apply to other people.” / «اولویت‌های من سزاوار استثناست؛ این محدودیت باید برای دیگران باشد.» |

Explain grandiosity in ordinary language as an exaggerated claim to importance or superiority. Do not equate confidence, ambition, leadership, cultural directness, or disability-related accommodations with the schema. Equal dignity does not require identical arrangements; an agreed accommodation can be fair without being a rigid claim to superiority.

Introduce **Arman / آرمان**, an original fictional adult from the domains overview. A shared studio allows two slots per member each week. Arman thinks his project is more important and takes a third slot despite knowing that two other members also need access. This introduces a reported interpretation and action; it does not imply that a booking dispute alone proves a schema.

## 3 Core emotional needs and healthy alternatives

### 3.1 Which core emotional needs may be unmet or frustrated?

Name Young et al.'s five broad need groupings: secure attachment; autonomy, competence, and identity; freedom to express valid needs and emotions; spontaneity and play; realistic limits and self-control. Select only relevant needs for substantive discussion. “Hurt” means that a legitimate need has been insufficiently supported or is frustrated; it does not mean the person is defective. The associations between needs and schemas are clinical formulations, not experimentally established individual causes. [1](#ref-1) [4](#ref-4)

**Original fictional examples:** the rendered guide uses Nima, Leila, and Omid to illustrate these three selected needs. The other two broad needs are named within the framework without treating all five as necessary explanations for this schema.

| Need within the framework | Possible relevance | Fictional frustration and meaning | Support without granting automatic exceptions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Realistic limits and self-control — central | Warm, consistent boundaries support waiting, sharing, respecting others' rights, and tolerating ordinary frustration. | Nima protests and repeatedly receives the whole game rather than taking turns; he may learn “Wanting it means I should have it now.” | Acknowledge disappointment, keep a fair turn-taking agreement, and help the person choose what to do while waiting. For the adult, check the booking policy and use an agreed request or waiting list. |
| Secure attachment, including acceptance — context-dependent | A person may need reliable care that is neither withdrawn after disagreement nor dependent on exceptional status. | Leila's ordinary worries are ignored while being “the best” brings attention; she may wonder whether ordinary experience is welcome. This is one possible history, not a hidden cause assumed for every reader. | Offer interest and warmth without ranking people; acknowledge feelings while retaining mutual limits. |
| Freedom to express valid needs and emotions — context-dependent | A wish, disappointment, or anger can be expressed without turning it into a demand or abandoning it altogether. | Omid has learned that asking for comfort is weak and demands that someone cancel their plans rather than saying he would like support. | Name the request clearly, ask what is possible, listen to the answer, and negotiate through a shared process; the need can remain valid even when that particular request is refused. |

Include at least one fully worked need/support example in the visible needs discussion: **situation → interpretation → need → supportive response**, with a mixed result. Separate the need for a workable limit from the strategy of obtaining a particular room, reservation, favor, or exception. Refer developmental discussion to section 5 rather than making the needs section an origins inventory.

### 3.2 A healthier, more flexible alternative

Compare rigid exceptionalism with self-respect and reciprocity across requests, response to “no,” attention to others, use of rules, responsibility, and repair. Flexible responses allow disappointment and advocacy, ask whether a rule is appropriate, and consider a fair process. They do not require accepting unfair treatment, giving up necessary care, or putting everyone else first.

**Original alternative — EN:** “My needs matter, and other people's needs also count. I can ask clearly and use a fair process without assuming a right to their agreement.”

**FA:** «نیازهای من مهم‌اند و نیازهای دیگران هم اهمیت دارند. می‌توانم روشن درخواست کنم و از روشی منصفانه استفاده کنم، بدون اینکه موافقت دیگران را حق قطعی خودم بدانم.»

## 4 Recognizing the pattern

### 4.1 Visible signs / نشانه‌های آشکار

Possible observations include repeatedly pressing for exceptions, taking more shared time than agreed, dismissing somebody else's contribution, or treating a refusal as an affront. Separate the observation from the meaning. Arman's extra booking is observable; “My project makes me exempt” is his reported interpretation. Irritation, urgency, or jaw tension are possible cues, not schema-specific symptoms.

### 4.2 Hidden or masked signs / نشانه‌های پنهان یا پوشیده

Use an original fictional contrast: someone appears generous or agreeable but privately feels that ordinary reciprocal needs would make them selfish, and exhausts themselves proving they never want special treatment. If its function is a rigid opposite of an entitlement belief, this can illustrate overcompensation; helpfulness alone cannot establish that formulation. Another person may avoid joining a group because being an ordinary equal member feels intolerable. Compare this with genuine lack of interest, access barriers, workload, or a considered decision not to participate.

Keep visible and masked categories educational and tentative. Readers may conclude that neither fits. “Hidden” does not mean disagreement confirms the pattern. A respectful accommodation request, a challenge to discrimination, or a firm response to unfair treatment can be healthy self-advocacy.

### 4.3 Common triggers and early cues / محرک‌های رایج و نشانه‌های اولیه

Explore waiting, shared limits, not being chosen, corrective feedback, another person's different wishes, and being expected to make an ordinary contribution. Distinguish an event such as a booking limit from its interpretation as disrespect or an unacceptable reduction in status. Allow cultural expectations, emergencies, genuine inequity, actual authority/responsibility, capacity, disability, and caregiving to change the meaning of a situation.

## 5 Possible origins and unmet needs

Describe qualified conceptual possibilities: insufficient consistent limits, overindulgence, modeled superiority or domination, excessive rescue from ordinary responsibilities, or an entitlement response that functions as compensation for another painful schema. Young et al. explicitly distinguish different possible pathways; do not turn one into a universal childhood account. [1](#ref-1)

Link these possibilities to the named needs in section 3. Effective limits can coexist with warmth and developmentally appropriate support. A need for realistic limits does not justify harshness, humiliation, or neglect. Present circumstances, learned social advantages, temperament, and context can also matter; a self-help example cannot establish their relative contributions. Do not require memory retrieval or retrospective blame.

## 6 How the cycle continues

Use Arman's original fictional studio example and exactly six visible steps, with optional explanations expanded through native disclosures:

1. **Trigger:** Arman sees the calendar and a coordinator's reminder that each member can book two slots per week. He knows two other members need access.
2. **Meaning or rule:** “My project is more important; the limit should not apply to me.” He notices irritation and a tight jaw, with a possible Impulsive/Undisciplined Child state. Do not infer hidden shame.
3. **Coping response:** he books a third slot to secure additional time, following the entitlement belief. This is surrender to this schema even if the outward behavior looks forceful.
4. **Immediate effect:** extra reserved time and satisfaction; the anticipated practical gain reinforces the response. Do not imply he already used the slot before the coordinator removes it.
5. **Later cost or shift:** another member objects, the coordinator removes the extra booking, and the member says the bookings cannot be relied on. Arman becomes irritated and then stops replying, a possible Detached Protector response avoiding the exchange.
6. **Reinforcement:** he concludes he should secure extra slots before anyone objects next time, without examining the rule or its effect on others.

Keep the complete version in section 6 and link from section 7. The Healthy Adult interruption occurs **before the next third booking**: “My frustration is real, and it does not cancel another member's equal claim.” Keep two slots and ask about the approved procedure for unused slots. **Mixed outcome:** no extra time is available that week; Arman shortens his project plan, remains irritated, and preserves others' access. Later repair does not guarantee restored trust. The completed reflection records the original loop; the experiment records this later attempt rather than rewriting the past.

## 7 Coping styles and modes

### 7.1 How the concepts relate

Distinguish **schema / طرحواره** (the enduring meaning), **coping style / سبک مقابله‌ای** (the broad relationship to that meaning), **coping response / پاسخ مقابله‌ای** (a particular action or mental strategy), and **mode / ذهنیت طرحواره‌ای** (the temporary state active at a moment). These are related but not interchangeable; modes can overlap or shift and are not identities. [1](#ref-1) [3](#ref-3) [4](#ref-4)

**Original optional prompt:** “What meaning was activated, what state was I in, and what was my response trying to achieve?” / «چه معنایی فعال شد، در چه حالتی بودم و پاسخم قرار بود چه چیزی را فراهم کند؟»

### 7.2 Three ways of coping with this schema

Young et al.'s entitlement row supplies the conceptual distinction: imposing one's wishes can surrender to entitlement; avoiding situations that lack an exceptional role can avoid activation; excessive attention to others' needs can rigidly counter it. The following situations are original illustrations, not assessment items. [1](#ref-1)

| Style | Trigger and meaning | Response and function | Cost or repetition | Flexible alternative |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Surrender / تسلیم | Arman encounters the ordinary booking limit: “My project deserves more.” | Takes extra slots, following the claim as true; gains access or control. | Others lose access; conflict reinforces the idea that he must push harder. | State the request, check the common process, and share access. |
| Avoidance / اجتناب | Leila likes a volunteer project but learns that duties are shared: “I do not want to be limited or asked to contribute.” | Stops answering messages to avoid reciprocal responsibility and the discomfort of accepting it. | Loses cooperation and a wanted opportunity. | Check capacity, offer a feasible contribution, or decline clearly and respectfully. |
| Overcompensation / جبران افراطی | Nader notices an urge for special treatment: “To prove I am not special, I must never want anything for myself.” | Gives away every suitable slot even when entitled to a fair share; rigid self-denial reverses the entitlement rule. | Exhaustion and resentment preserve an all-or-nothing view. | Accept a fair share for himself and others. |

Do not label all forceful or grandiose behavior overcompensation. Its function can be surrender to this schema or compensation for a different schema, such as Defectiveness/Shame. Likewise, generous behavior may be a freely chosen act of care. Seek the individual's meaning and actual circumstances before choosing a tentative formulation. [1](#ref-1)

### 7.3 Modes that may become active

Use the project glossary; select modes relevant to the narrative rather than treating every mode family as required. Each table row must include an activation cue, an original internal sentence, possible feelings/body/actions, and a need or difficulty with a concrete Healthy Adult response. The Young–First listing and consulted Arntz–Jacob excerpt support model terms; original recognition cues are not diagnostic criteria. [3](#ref-3) [4](#ref-4)

The guide selects six states. These are **original fictional illustrations** of the cited framework; the reader is not required to identify with every row.

| Mode | Activation cue and original inner sentence | Feelings/body/actions | Need or difficulty and a Healthy Adult response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Impulsive/Undisciplined Child / کودک تکانشی/بی‌انضباط | Preferred access is unavailable: “I want it now; I should not have to wait.” | Impatience, tight jaw, restlessness; taking an extra share or pressing until someone yields. | Realistic limits and frustration tolerance: “I can be disappointed without taking another person's turn.” Pause and choose an allowed option. |
| Detached Protector / محافظ جداشده | A message describes an action's impact: “If I disengage, I will not have to feel this.” | Numbness, distance, weariness; leaving the message unread and changing activity. | Manageable contact and responsibility: name a time to return, send a brief reply, and examine the specific impact. |
| Vulnerable Child / کودک آسیب‌پذیر | After disagreement, the person actually reports “I am afraid I no longer have a place in the group.” | Sadness, fear, lump in throat, tears; seeking support or shrinking back. This is not inferred from grandiosity. | Acceptance and belonging with responsibility: “One harmful choice is not my entire worth.” Seek support and repair without demanding forgiveness. |
| Overcompensator / جبران‌گر افراطی | In a different formulation, a reported fear of low worth is activated: “I must prove superiority so nobody sees my weakness.” | Tension, vigilance; exaggerated achievements and devaluing another person's contribution. | This can compensate for Defectiveness/Shame, not entitlement itself. Support worth without diminishing another person; explore underlying feelings only if present. |
| Punitive Parent / والد تنبیه‌گر | After a mistake: “I am a bad person and deserve no support.” | Global shame, heaviness; self-deprivation or abandoning repair. | Specific responsibility without humiliation: release the extra booking and apologize; self-punishment is not repair. |
| Healthy Adult / بزرگسال سالم | Notices the exemption rule or urge to disengage: “My wishes and others' rights both matter.” | Can remain angry or upset while checking facts, listening, and separating request from automatic right. | Preserve a fair share, use agreed negotiations, express needs, and repair harmful actions. |

“Child” and “Parent” are model terms, not insults or proof of maturity, parenting, or history. Healthy Adult combines care, responsibility, and limits rather than requiring uninterrupted calm. Anger alone does not establish Angry Child: the listing distinguishes frustration of core needs from immediate wants and impatience in Impulsive/Undisciplined Child. A behavioral limit differs from punitive global condemnation.

### 7.4 A recurring sequence and a different branch

Link to the complete six-step Arman cycle. Return to the same booking trigger for an avoidance branch: rather than taking extra slots, Arman avoids the studio because an ordinary place feels unacceptable. This reduces exposure to an equal role while costing access and contact; it is an alternative possibility rather than a mandatory next stage.

Place the Healthy Adult interruption at the urge to take an extra slot: **“It is disappointing to have less time. My project matters, and the others' access matters too.” / «داشتن زمان کمتر ناامیدکننده است. پروژهٔ من مهم است و دسترسی دیگران هم اهمیت دارد.»** Ask about the agreed options, retain an ordinary booking, and accept that another person may still be displeased after the earlier incident. Do not make immediate forgiveness or emotional relief a success condition.

End with permission for a different or uncertain sequence: **“Does any part fit, does your sequence differ, or are you unsure?” / «آیا بخشی از این توالی با تجربهٔ شما جور است، توالی شما متفاوت است، یا مطمئن نیستید؟»**

## 8 Everyday examples

Use the following original fictional contexts without assuming other people's motives or making anybody responsible for mistreatment directed at them:

- **Mina and her partner — complete worked case:** they have agreed that each has an evening for a personal activity. Mina assumes her partner should cover the household tasks because her day was harder. Impatience and pressure follow the entitlement belief; her partner cancels the planned activity, so Mina gains rest while trust suffers. Dismissing the partner as “too sensitive” could reinforce “pressure gets me what I need.” She needs rest and a workable shared plan, not automatic priority. A later repair acknowledges the specific impact, asks what would help, and covers the agreed tasks during a replacement evening chosen by the partner. The partner accepts but remains upset; Mina gives up another preferred plan and is still disappointed. She keeps the agreement. Repair does not create a right to immediate forgiveness.
- **Reza and family:** earning more money becomes a claim to exemption from ordinary household responsibilities; actual capacity and agreed roles still matter.
- **Sara and friendship:** expectations of immediate replies strain a friend's available time and boundaries.
- **Neda and work:** rigid overgiving tries to prove the opposite of wanting special treatment; generosity could also be a freely chosen response, so its function must be explored.
- **Amir and a study group:** high grades become a claim to superior status or control rather than one contribution among others.
- **Darya — healthy advocacy counterexample:** an access need requires additional setup time. Asking for a fair adjustment is not schema evidence merely because arrangements differ.

Mina's case supplies the second complete sequence: observable event, meaning, possible state, response/function, immediate and later effects, need, alternative, and mixed result. Keep the six-step Arman story in section 6 rather than duplicating it here.

## 9 Related patterns

Use one observable situation, such as requesting additional studio time, to contrast different possible meanings. Similar behavior cannot choose the formulation or establish a diagnosis. More than one schema may be relevant; a conceptual comparison does not establish an empirical correlation. Cite the definitions. [1](#ref-1) [2](#ref-2)

| Related schema | Possible overlap | Different central meaning in the same situation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Insufficient Self-Control / Self-Discipline](../../Interactive%20Guide%20to%20the%20Insufficient%20Self%20Control%20Self%20Discipline%20Schema.html) | Difficulty accepting a limit or waiting. | Entitlement: “The limit should not apply to me.” Insufficient self-control: “I accept the limit, but the waiting/discomfort feels hard to tolerate.” Both may occur. |
| Approval-Seeking / Recognition-Seeking | Pursuing an exceptional role or status. | Entitlement: “I deserve priority.” Approval-Seeking: “Their admiration will show that I matter.” |
| [Defectiveness / Shame](../../Interactive%20Guide%20to%20the%20Defectiveness%20Shame%20Schema.html) | Defensive superiority may appear similar. | Entitlement: “I am more deserving.” Defectiveness-related compensation: “If I look ordinary, they will discover something unacceptable about me.” Do not assume this underlying fear. |
| [Unrelenting Standards / Hypercriticalness](../../Interactive%20Guide%20to%20the%20Unrelenting%20Standards%20Hypercriticalness%20Schema.html) | Pressing for extra time or controlling how a task is done. | Entitlement: “My work earns me an exception.” Unrelenting Standards: “The work must meet an exacting standard; stopping is unacceptable.” |

Include a brief distinction between this schema formulation and psychiatric diagnoses. The guide does not diagnose narcissistic personality disorder or infer it from a behavior.

## 10 Examining your experience

Provide a few original, optional recognition prompts about the meaning of limits, reciprocal standards, listening to the impact of a request, and whether an exception would feel fair if another person received it. Do not assign scores or reproduce questionnaire items. Preserve exactly eight shared reflection textareas, bilingual labels/helper prompts, immediate print mirrors, independent clearing, and accessible status feedback.

**Original fictional completed reflection:** use Arman's initial studio incident, before any later experiment. Keep the final wording synchronized with the full story in section 6.

| Standard field | Intended example content and optional mode/function prompt |
| --- | --- |
| 1. What happened, and what triggered the reaction? | The calendar and coordinator reminded me of two slots per member per week. I wanted a third and knew two other members needed time. |
| 2. What did the situation seem to mean? What rule appeared? | “My project matters more; the limit should not apply to me.” Include the earliest internal sentence. |
| 3. What feelings and bodily sensations did I notice? | Irritation and a tight jaw; possible Impulsive/Undisciplined Child. After the objection, I became irritated and stopped replying, a possible Detached Protector shift. Names remain optional. |
| 4. What did I do, and what was that response trying to achieve? | I booked a third slot to get extra project time, following the exceptional-rights belief; possible surrender to entitlement. Later I stopped replying to avoid the exchange. |
| 5. What happened immediately, and what were the later costs? | I initially reserved extra time and felt satisfied. After a member objected, the coordinator removed the extra slot; the member said the bookings could not be relied on. I thought I should secure extras before anyone objected next time. |
| 6. What did I need in that situation? | A workable plan, room to express disappointment, and realistic reciprocal limits; needing project time does not establish a right to somebody else's slot. |
| 7. What alternative could respect my needs and the situation? | Acknowledge frustration, check fair options, request time through the agreed process, and correct any extra booking. |
| 8. What happened next, or what support would help? | In this first loop I stopped replying and did not try the alternative. Next time, the urge to claim a third slot can cue a pause and a check with the coordinator about agreed options. |

## 11 Practicing change

Offer small, reversible practices in a manageable setting: notice the wish and the other person's stake before asking; check a shared limit and ask through the agreed process; make one respectful request and allow a refusal; acknowledge a specific impact and offer a repair. Supplementary CCI assertiveness guidance supports communication that respects one's own and others' rights; it is not evidence that this guide changes a schema. [7](#ref-7)

Retain genuine safety, care, access, work, and legal responsibilities. The experiment does not require relinquishing needed accommodations, accepting inequity, entering coercive conflict, or compensating through excessive self-sacrifice. Pause and seek suitable support if the situation is escalating or the agreement is unclear.

Use exactly six shared planner textareas and a completed original fictional **later** studio experiment:

| Standard field | Intended content |
| --- | --- |
| Situation and limits | The next ordinary studio booking: two slots per member, no emergency or unmet accommodation request; use the agreed reservation process. |
| Prediction | A specific observable expectation about what would happen without claiming an exception, plus the anticipated urge to insist or withdraw. Do not make the prediction merely “I will feel disappointed.” |
| Small action | Before booking a third slot, pause, keep two, and ask the coordinator about the approved unused-slot procedure. |
| Support or stopping conditions | A prepared Healthy Adult sentence; consult the coordinator if rules or access needs are unclear; stop an escalating argument and return to a workable channel. |
| Observed outcome | No extra slot was available this week. Arman shortened his project plan, remained irritated, and left others' access intact. This does not establish immediate repair of trust. |
| Learning and next adjustment | Compare observation with the prediction, notice whether the insist/withdraw sequence changed, and adjust one next step. An imperfect or unfinished attempt can still provide information. |

The full guide should make the testable prediction and reported result match. A useful outcome is more choice and reciprocal action, not guaranteed agreement, special access, praise, or absence of anger.

## 12 How therapy can help

Describe collaborative assessment of meanings, context, coping functions, modes, strengths, consequences, and alternative explanations. Discuss cognitive examination of rules and reciprocity; behavioral experiments with manageable limits and requests; relational work on feedback, boundaries, and repair; therapist-supported experiential work such as imagery or chair dialogues when appropriate. Do not turn intensive experiential methods into independent instructions. [1](#ref-1) [3](#ref-3) [4](#ref-4)

### When professional support may help / چه زمانی کمک حرفه‌ای می‌تواند مفید باشد؟

Offer guidance for repeated conflict over limits, difficulty hearing impact, persistent disruption of work or relationships, distress or self-condemnation after incidents, or reflection that becomes overwhelming. The reader can seek help without first adopting the schema label. Assessment should consider real unfairness, access needs, cultural and situational expectations, other patterns, and the person's goals. A whole-treatment trial cannot establish that every reader needs the same approach. [6](#ref-6)

## 13 Reviewing progress

Use observable, personally chosen directions: noticing the exemption rule sooner; listening to another person's account; making a clear request; tolerating a refusal without intimidation or withdrawal; honoring shared limits; acknowledging a specific harm; offering a proportionate repair; and returning after a setback. Noticing afterward is useful. Do not count modes or set a recovery deadline.

| Realistic direction | Unrealistic demand |
| --- | --- |
| Respect a limit while disappointed. | Never feel frustrated by “no.” |
| Notice an exception-seeking urge, sometimes after acting. | Detect every mode before it happens. |
| Offer a concrete correction and listen. | Obtain immediate forgiveness or proof of being a good person. |
| Make a fair request without excessive giving. | Prove that one never needs anything from others. |

After a setback, separate responsibility for an action from condemnation of the whole person. Identify the next repair or support that is realistically available. Place the closing reflection here so references remain last.

**Original closing prompt — EN:** “Where did I make a little more room for both my needs and another person's rights, and what would help me try again?”

**FA:** «کجا کمی فضای بیشتری هم برای نیازهای خودم و هم برای حقوق دیگری ایجاد کردم، و چه چیزی به تلاش بعدی کمک می‌کند؟»

## 14 What research supports

Separate the evidence categories and preserve the access limitations from the [evidence record](../research/entitlement-grandiosity-sources.md):

- **Conceptual sources [1–4]:** descriptions of the schema, domain, needs framework, coping, and modes guide a formulation. They do not establish any individual's developmental cause or validate original guide prompts. The needs mapping is heuristic.
- **Schema-related measurement/observational research [5]:** Pauwels et al. examined the Young Schema Questionnaire short form in 672 Flemish community adults. In an age/gender-adjusted model including multiple schemas, Entitlement scores were associated with lower Agreeableness. Self-report, cross-sectional measurement and one community sample cannot establish a motive, diagnosis, cause, or treatment effect. Do not treat a personality-trait association as a judgment of the reader.
- **Whole-treatment evidence [6]:** Bamelis et al.'s randomized trial included 323 adults with specified personality disorders and compared schema therapy, clarification-oriented psychotherapy, and usual treatment. Diagnostic recovery favored schema therapy, but self-report outcomes did not show between-treatment differences. This is evidence about a whole treatment in that population, not an Entitlement-only intervention or this interactive guide; abstract/metadata access limits the summary.
- **Supplementary guidance [7]:** CCI's assertiveness material informs clear communication and respect for mutual rights. It does not test this schema guide.

Do not substitute research on a differently defined psychological-entitlement construct for measurement of the Young schema without explicitly naming that distinction. Do not imply that these original worksheets are a validated treatment or assessment.
