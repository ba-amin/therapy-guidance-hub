**Clinical emphasis:** give balanced attention to seeking acceptance by pleasing and seeking admiration through achievement, appearance, image, or status. Their shared issue is allowing others' responses to determine worth and choices at the expense of a more stable sense of self and one's own needs. Wanting affection, appreciation, credit, feedback, or recognition is not itself a schema. Do not prescribe universal indifference to others' views, infer a personality disorder, or treat ordinary courtesy, cultural obligations, necessary workplace feedback, access needs, or actual danger as pathology.

## 2 Understanding this schema

### Schema at a glance / طرحواره در یک نگاه

| Required item | Content to preserve in both languages |
| --- | --- |
| Brief description | An excessive reliance on approval, attention, or recognition can make self-worth and important choices depend on other people's reactions rather than one's own preferences, needs, and values. Seeking acceptance and seeking admiration can look different while serving a related function. [1](#ref-1) [2](#ref-2) |
| Domain | **Other-Directedness / دیگرجهت‌مندی**; link to [the overview's domain entry](../../Guide%20to%20Schema%20Domains%20and%20Recognition.html#domain-other-directedness). Introduce the overview's fictional **Omid / امید**: he prefers a quiet creative project but chooses a prestigious committee at a voluntary local club because other people praise the role. He keeps checking reactions despite disliking the work. The setting is a discretionary activity; financial survival or mandatory job duties are not being reduced to a schema example.

Explain that the English label includes both approval and recognition; the established Persian title is an editorial translation. Neither publicity nor modesty, social-media use nor privacy, proves the pattern. Healthy ambition and enjoyment of praise can coexist with personal choice.

## 3 Core emotional needs and healthy alternatives

### 3.1 Which core emotional needs may be unmet or frustrated?

Name Young et al.'s five broad groupings: secure attachment; autonomy, competence, and identity; freedom to express valid needs and emotions; spontaneity and play; realistic limits and self-control. “Unmet” or “frustrated” describes insufficient support for a legitimate need, not a damaged person. The following selected needs are conceptual applications, not a causal account established for every reader. [1](#ref-1) [4](#ref-4)

| Selected need | Everyday meaning and possible frustration | Original fictional support example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Freedom to express valid needs and emotions — central to the formulation | A person can say what they prefer, need, or feel without having to earn permission by pleasing. Leila is praised for being easygoing while her tiredness is dismissed; she may learn that acceptance requires concealing a valid limit. | Leila names a modest limit and negotiates with a friend, leaving room for both people's wishes. Expression does not guarantee that a particular request can be met. |
| Autonomy, competence, and identity — when admired options displace preferences | A person can develop preferences, try interests, and evaluate choices using both relevant feedback and their own experience. A hypothetical history in which Omid's interests are overlooked while family reputation receives attention illustrates one possible frustration, not an established cause. | Omid's interest in a quiet creative task receives serious attention even when somebody else prefers a more prestigious role. He can try a limited contribution and assess whether he values it. |
| Secure attachment, including acceptance — when connection feels conditional | Care and interest remain available when a person is ordinary, disagrees, or does not impress. Nima mainly receives warmth when winning or entertaining and may wonder whether ordinary experience is welcome. | A supportive person offers interest and warmth without first asking for achievement or entertainment; care does not require unanimous agreement. |

Give every selected need a concrete fictional situation, possible meaning, and supportive response in the rendered guide. For the complete worked example, Leila is tired and asks to attend dinner for one hour. Her friend wishes she could stay longer but listens; she leaves as agreed and still feels uneasy. The need for expression is supported without requiring universal approval or immediate calm. Keep the dedicated needs discussion separate from developmental possibilities in section 5. Do not introduce “authenticity” or “validation” as additional formal categories attributed to Young.

### 3.2 A healthier, more flexible alternative

Compare flexible appreciation with approval dependence: enjoying praise without requiring it to confirm worth; asking for specific task feedback rather than global reassurance; choosing a worthwhile interest even without an audience; expressing a preference while remaining considerate; receiving criticism without automatically treating it as a verdict on the whole person.

The alternative is not social isolation or refusal to consider others. Maintain actual professional requirements, collaborative obligations, safety, and respect. A difficult reaction can matter and deserve a response without becoming the sole measure of a person's worth.

## 4 Recognizing the pattern

### 4.1 Visible signs / نشانه‌های آشکار

Possible outward actions include changing an expressed opinion to fit the audience, repeatedly seeking reassurance after a satisfactory interaction, choosing a status-bearing role despite low interest, or monitoring praise and recognition at the expense of the task. Separate observable actions from their possible meaning. Omid joining a committee is observable; the rule that admiration proves his worth comes from his fictional reported experience.

### 4.2 Hidden or masked signs / نشانه‌های پنهان یا پوشیده

Show a person who looks confident, accomplished, agreeable, or independent while privately checking whether they impressed or pleased people. Their response may briefly reduce uncertainty but displace a preference or sustain monitoring. Another person may avoid visibility to avoid discovering that approval is absent. Appearance alone cannot establish the motive or coping style.

Include a healthy counterexample and an alternative explanation. A person may seek accurate feedback to meet a genuine work requirement, dress formally for an agreed occasion, enjoy recognition, or preserve privacy by choice. “Hidden” must never mean that disagreeing with the guide confirms a problem.

### 4.3 Common triggers and early cues / محرک‌های رایج و نشانه‌های اولیه

Possible triggers include comparison, a visible achievement, silence after sharing news, another person's different preference, criticism, or an opportunity carrying status. Possible cues include scanning faces, rehearsing a pleasing answer, a tight stomach, excitement followed by uncertainty, or an urge to check reactions. Treat these as original illustrations, not diagnostic signs. Allow cultural expectations, discrimination, economic dependence, coercion, and real consequences to change the formulation.

## 5 Possible origins and unmet needs

Describe qualified conceptual possibilities: approval made contingent on meeting others' expectations, emphasis on appearance or status, and limited support for a child's own preferences and identity. Relate these to the selected needs in section 3. There may have been warmth as well as pressure; no particular family history is necessary or proved by current behavior. [1](#ref-1) [2](#ref-2) [4](#ref-4)

Discuss ongoing reinforcement through comparison, praise, or social expectations without turning an observational study into a causal explanation. Social-media or professional incentives can encourage attention to reactions without establishing this schema. Do not prompt recovered memories or retrospective blame.

## 6 How the cycle continues

Expand Omid's original fictional club example using six native-disclosure steps:

1. **Trigger:** a voluntary local arts club offers a prestigious committee role while Omid is interested in quietly making a small artwork; people praise the committee role.
2. **Meaning or rule:** “Their admiration will show that I matter.” His chest tightens, he feels anxious, and he pressures himself to impress; mode labels remain tentative.
3. **Coping response:** he takes the role despite his preference and repeatedly checks reactions. The function is to secure recognition as evidence of worth, illustrating surrender to this schema.
4. **Immediate effect:** praise briefly reassures him; relief need not last and does not prove that the choice matches his interests.
5. **Later cost or shift:** committee work and checking group-message reactions consume time for the artwork; he later blames himself harshly for not appearing impressive enough, suggesting a possible shift into Punitive Parent.
6. **Reinforcement:** he concludes that he must maintain a more impressive image or obtain another response to keep feeling worthwhile, making the next visible choice harder to evaluate on its own merits.

Place a specific Healthy Adult interruption before an optional next commitment: “I can appreciate their praise and still ask which contribution I actually want.” Name a small action, the need it serves, and a mixed outcome. Existing commitments are honored or renegotiated responsibly; the guide does not recommend dropping duties impulsively.

## 7 Coping styles and modes

### 7.1 How the concepts relate

Distinguish the enduring **schema**, broad **coping style**, particular **coping response**, and temporary **mode** using Omid's situation. Seeking admiration is a theme; choosing the prestigious role and checking reactions are responses; following the approval rule is surrender; a vulnerable, pressured, or compliant state can be active during the sequence. A person can shift states or use different coping styles. [1](#ref-1) [3](#ref-3) [4](#ref-4)

### 7.2 Three ways of coping with this schema

Use separate explicitly fictional examples with trigger, meaning, immediate function, later cost, and flexible alternative:

- **Surrender / تسلیم:** pleasing an audience or selecting a prestigious activity to obtain approval follows the schema's rule. Brief affirmation can reinforce the response while personal interests become less clear.
- **Avoidance / اجتناب:** Leila leaves an optional invitation to meet an admired artist unanswered because she fears seeming ordinary. Avoidance briefly eases anxiety but loses a chance for connection and examining the expectation. A short conversation about a real interest may be manageable when she has capacity and safety; declining because of exhaustion can be healthy.
- **Overcompensation / جبران افراطی:** Nader disparages an idea he actually likes to provoke disapproval and prove that the group's opinion does not matter. He briefly feels in control, but connection and his own preference both suffer; others' reactions still organize the choice. Stating his actual view respectfully is a flexible alternative. Deliberate disagreement and healthy boundaries are not automatically overcompensation.

Explain why the function fits. Status-seeking can be surrender to Approval-Seeking even when it looks bold; it is not automatically overcompensation. The same action might compensate for another schema or reflect ordinary ambition, so do not classify by appearance. [1](#ref-1)

### 7.3 Modes that may become active

Use the project glossary and the following explicitly fictional activation examples. Mode definitions come from the conceptual listing; these specific cues, dialogue, and responses are original teaching illustrations rather than assessment criteria. [3](#ref-3)

| Possible mode | Activation and original inner sentence | Feelings, body, and action | Need or difficulty and flexible response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vulnerable Child / کودک آسیب‌پذیر | A friend disagrees: “If I am not approved of, do I still matter?” | Worry, chest tightness, feeling small, urge to repeatedly ask whether the friend is upset. | Acceptance and connection: acknowledge the hurt, distinguish one disagreement from whole-person worth, ask once for clarification or seek support without requiring a permanent guarantee. |
| Compliant Surrenderer / تسلیم‌شوندهٔ مطیع | A partner proposes a plan: “Agreeing without difficulty will make me more likable.” | Smiling with stomach tension, concealing a preference behind “Whatever you want.” | Expression and identity: ask for time before answering and state one modest preference; cooperation can include one's own voice. |
| Detached Protector / محافظ جداشده | Showing work to an admired person feels exposing: “None of this matters to me.” | Numbness or distraction, closing the conversation and spending a long time in unrelated content. | Manageable contact with feeling: acknowledge worry, choose a limited break, then decide about one small step rather than making disengagement compulsory. |
| Overcompensator / جبران‌گر افراطی | Others praise a taste or preference: “I will show that their opinion means nothing.” | Tension, combative energy, deliberately disparaging a view even when it matches one's own. | Autonomy with connection: acknowledge the wish to be liked, stop the attack, recognize its impact, and state the actual preference respectfully. |
| Demanding Parent / والد پرتوقع | Before a meeting: “You must always be interesting, successful, and liked.” | Pressure, tense shoulders, endless rehearsal, neglecting rest. | Proportionate preparation and expression of limits: identify what the actual meeting requires; universal approval is not a duty. |
| Punitive Parent / والد تنبیه‌گر | No fresh praise: “You are worthless; you do not deserve enjoyment.” | Shame and heaviness, cancelling an enjoyable plan solely as punishment. | Acceptance alongside responsibility: replace humiliation with a specific behavioral observation and make relevant repair if an error occurred. |
| Healthy Adult / بزرگسال سالم | Before an automatic answer: “I enjoy approval; my wishes and responsibilities matter too.” | May remain anxious while pausing, gathering necessary information, and separating observations from guesses. | Expression, identity, and connection: state a preference, consider useful feedback, and honor or responsibly renegotiate commitments. |

Distinguish pressure to be admired from punishment for failing to impress. A caring response may acknowledge wanting appreciation, identify a valid preference, tolerate discomfort, and choose a proportionate action. Healthy Adult is not a requirement to be calm, indifferent, or universally approved of. “Child” and “Parent” are model terms, not identities or evidence of specific childhood events.

The alternative is a possibility, not the next mandatory stage. Genuine exhaustion could explain the same outward action.

## 8 Everyday examples

Include both forms of the pattern across family, partnership, friendship, work/study, and public recognition or online contexts. Preserve a healthy counterexample of chosen cooperation or feedback-seeking without making emotional independence the ideal.

Use **Mina / مینا** for a complete second fictional case in a close relationship. Her partner prefers an optional all-day weekend outing; Mina wants a shorter shared visit and quiet time. She agrees to secure approval, her partner says they are pleased, and she feels briefly reassured before resentment and distance develop. The repair identifies her response without assuming the partner demanded it: “I said yes before explaining what I wanted. Could we consider both preferences?” They negotiate a shorter shared portion and independent activity later. Her partner is disappointed about changing the plan but agrees; neither receives the entire original preference. Closeness can recover without a guarantee of immediate approval or relief. Neither schema language nor a repair script makes the reader responsible for mistreatment.

Every complete case connects observable situation, possible meaning/state, response/function, immediate effect and later cost, need, alternative, and learning. Label all scenarios fictional and original.

## 11 Practicing change

Offer three manageable original practices: notice one preference before consulting; express a small difference respectfully; and separate specific task feedback from whole-person worth. The experiment below applies these principles to a limited, personally meaningful activity without making praise the outcome. Supplementary assertiveness guidance may inform the examples, but the original exercises are not validated schema interventions. [8](#ref-8)

Keep the six fields: situation and limits; prediction; small action; support or stopping conditions; observed outcome; learning and next adjustment. Preserve real obligations, employment requirements, safety, and relational context. Independent practices stay modest; intensive imagery and chair work belong in therapist-supported discussion.

**Completed fictional experiment:** after completing existing club duties, Omid offers a small artwork for the next optional activity rather than automatically choosing the prestigious role. He predicts that the organizer will reject the artwork unless he joins the committee and expects a tight chest, anxiety, and an urge to reverse his choice or repeatedly ask whether he is valued. He writes his preference, makes the offer, and checks practical size and deadline requirements. The organizer accepts and agrees those requirements; a friend still prefers the committee choice, and Omid remains uneasy. Learning: the specific rejection prediction did not occur in this encounter; universal approval was not obtained, and other people's private thoughts remain unknown. Next time he will use the tight-chest cue to pause before committing. Practical and emotional stopping/support conditions remain explicit.

## 12 How therapy can help

Describe collaborative assessment of current situations, recurring meanings, coping functions, modes, developmental hypotheses, alternative explanations, and actual constraints. Questionnaires can contribute to assessment but do not replace context or establish a personal cause. [1](#ref-1) [4](#ref-4)

Explain cognitive examination of approval rules, behavioral experiments that preserve responsibilities, relational attention to reassurance and disagreement within therapy, and therapist-supported experiential work. Do not give independent instructions for intensive imagery or chair work, or imply that this page reproduces a full treatment.

### When professional support may help / چه زمانی کمک حرفه‌ای می‌تواند مفید باشد؟

Suggest collaborative help when distress, repeated reassurance or reaction checking, difficulty expressing preferences, or patterns in relationships and everyday functioning persist, or when reflection feels overwhelming. A therapist can examine other explanations and adapt pace and support; no schema label or score is required before seeking help.

## 13 Reviewing progress

Give concrete contrasts: noticing a preference before asking others versus never wanting approval; tolerating a mixed reaction versus universal acceptance; using specific feedback versus rejecting all feedback; repairing an automatic agreement versus always saying the perfect thing; recovering after a checking episode versus never checking again.

Return to Omid after a setback. He may recognize the familiar rule only after volunteering; that can still create a chance to review the commitment responsibly, name his interest, and plan support for the next choice. Progress concerns greater choice, clearer preferences, honest negotiation, and recovery rather than a streak, mode count, permanent confidence, or removal of ordinary relational needs.

End clinical reflection here so references remain last.

## 14 What research supports

Preserve the separate evidence categories and exact access statements from the [evidence record](../research/approval-seeking-recognition-seeking-sources.md):

- **Conceptual sources [1–4]:** define the schema, domain, needs, coping, and modes. They support a formulation, not an established individual cause or a diagnosis from a sign list.
- **Measurement research [5]:** Saggino et al.'s Italian YSQ-L3 validation analysed 148 clinical and 918 community adults. Approval-Seeking internal consistency was high, but repeat-measurement reliability was below the authors' criterion; structural fit was imperfect and the specific-domain loading was nonsignificant in the clinical hierarchical model. A small retest sample, heterogeneous diagnoses, imputation, and language/context limit inference. It does not validate the guide's original prompts, Persian translation, diagnostic thresholds, or treatment effectiveness.
- **Schema-specific observational research [6]:** Cudo et al.'s cross-sectional study of 619 Polish Facebook users, ages 18–30, included 568 women and excluded people who played videogames in the preceding year. Approval-Seeking was positively associated with problematic Facebook use both in a correlation and the adjusted model. Self-report and the selected population limit generalization; association cannot establish direction, causation, or a treatment effect. The article's later four-domain grouping does not replace the guide's explicitly chosen Young/ISST five-domain teaching map.
- **Whole-treatment evidence [7]:** Bamelis et al.'s trial of 323 adults with specified personality disorders compares complete schema therapy with clarification-oriented psychotherapy and usual treatment. Preserve diagnostic recovery and the absence of between-treatment differences on self-report outcomes. Abstract/metadata access limits the summary; the trial does not test an Approval-Seeking-only intervention or this guide.
- **Supplementary guidance [8]:** CCI's assertiveness workbook informs communication practices, not claims that these original schema exercises have demonstrated efficacy.

Do not substitute related concepts such as contingent self-esteem, social desirability, narcissism, or generic reassurance-seeking for the measured Young schema without explaining the distinction. Keep research summaries proportionate to the passages actually consulted.
