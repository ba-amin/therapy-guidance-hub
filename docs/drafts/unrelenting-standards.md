# Unrelenting Standards / Hypercriticalness guide draft

Authoring outline for **معیارهای سرسختانه / عیب‌جویی افراطی**. Follow the [shared standard](../schema-guide-standard.md) and [reusable template](../schema-guide-template.md). The current guide largely follows this sequence: align labels and repeated structures while retaining verified material. This editorial draft includes original illustrations and exercises, not a clinical assessment.

## 1 Getting started

Offer the shared recognition, examination, and practice paths. Readers can stop after one useful observation. Original opening:

**EN:** “When finishing one task only reveals the next demand, it can help to examine the rules behind the pressure.”

**FA:** «وقتی تمام‌کردن یک کار فقط توقع بعدی را آشکار می‌کند، بررسی قواعد پشت این فشار می‌تواند کمک‌کننده باشد.»

## 2 Understanding this schema

Define damaging pressure to meet exceptionally high internal standards. Introduce perfectionism, rigid rules, and preoccupation with time and efficiency. [1](#ref-1)

Original core-rule example: **“I must keep improving; stopping means I am falling behind.”** / **«باید مدام بهتر شوم؛ توقف یعنی عقب‌افتادن.»** Explain that ambition alone does not establish this pattern.

## 3 Healthy needs and flexible alternatives

Use the shared comparison table: motivation, flexibility, mistakes, satisfaction, and costs. Illustrate demanding standards and flexible alternatives. Include competence alongside rest, enjoyment, connection, and realistic limits. Avoid implying that responsibility or achievement must be abandoned.

## 4 Recognizing the pattern

Organize optional observations by thoughts, emotions, body, actions, and triggers. Examples: unnecessary editing, irritation during interruptions, tension while resting, and postponing tasks whose ideal version feels unreachable. Consider workload and circumstances before attributing everything to a schema.

## 5 Possible origins and unmet needs

Explore remembered expectations about performance, mistakes, leisure, and usefulness across school, peers, family, and work. Ask what permission or support was missing. Keep origins tentative; high expectations do not establish abusive or unloving caregiving.

## 6 How the cycle continues

Place this fictional sequence in the shared six-step expandable cycle:

1. A routine presentation is due.
2. “No slide may have an imperfection” brings anxiety and urgency.
3. The person repeatedly polishes slides after the requirements are met.
4. Pressure briefly eases after more editing.
5. Sleep and time with others are lost.
6. They conclude that repeated polishing was necessary and set the same demand next time.

Offer an interruption: agree on purpose and stopping point beforehand. Keep the illustration readable without expanding every step.

## 7 Coping styles and modes

Classify coping by function: overworking may surrender to this schema, avoiding evaluation may evade it, and rigidly rejecting all standards may overcompensate; perfectionism can also compensate for shame. [2](#ref-2)

Differentiate demanding pressure, punitive condemnation, and Healthy Adult balance. [3](#ref-3) Use short original dialogues; avoid assigning a mode solely from observed behavior.

## 8 Everyday examples

Retain fictional work, study, parenting, relationship, household, and rest examples. Include a demand, cost, flexible alternative, and uncertainty.

**Worked example:** Amir has cleaned before friends arrive but feels that rest is allowed only after every cupboard is organized. He predicts he cannot enjoy the visit with anything unfinished. He stops when the shared rooms are comfortable and rests. Later, he records whether he could participate in the visit even while some discomfort remained.

## 9 Related patterns

Compare approval seeking, defectiveness/shame, failure, and punitiveness through feared consequences: disappointing oneself, losing approval, exposure, inability, or deserving punishment. Include overlap. Recognizing this schema does not establish a psychiatric diagnosis.

## 10 Examining your experience

Retain one shared worksheet input set. Original prompts: “What standard felt compulsory?” “Who actually required it?” “What did extra effort improve?” “What did it displace?” “What need was overlooked?” “What would enough mean here?” Use no scores or thresholds; permit skipping fields and switching language without losing answers.

## 11 Practicing change

Use a low-consequence experiment: set a reasonable time limit for formatting a personal invitation, then send it once the essential information is correct. Record prediction, outcome, effort, and learning. Preserve actual safety and care requirements. Add optional rest practice and a cost–benefit comparison; avoid making relaxation another achievement target.

## 12 How therapy can help

Outline collaborative assessment, examining rigid rules, behavioral change, and the therapeutic relationship. Present experiential methods as therapist-supported options; avoid requiring painful recollection or solo chair work. Connect seeking help to distress, interference, and personal preference.

## 13 Reviewing progress

Look for flexibility, earlier stopping, recovery from mistakes, and room for relationships. Invite observations, not performance metrics. Unfinished worksheets can coexist with progress. Returning to an old rule invites reflection, not a verdict of failure.

## 14 What research supports

Preserve the current guide's separation of conceptual explanations, observational associations, whole-treatment trials, and multicomponent digital interventions. Recheck each primary study and its limitations before carrying results forward. Label supplementary CBT guidance clearly. These sources should not become evidence that this page or its original exercises constitute a validated treatment.
