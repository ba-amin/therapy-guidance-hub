Authoring outline for **Abandonment / Instability — رهاشدگی / بی‌ثباتی**, following the [shared standard](../schema-guide-standard.md) and [fifteen-section template](../schema-guide-template.md). The [companion HTML](../../Interactive%20Guide%20to%20the%20Abandonment%20Instability%20Schema.html) addresses adults exploring their own experiences. Persian is the default; English and Persian must preserve the same meanings, examples, qualifications, and citation numbers. The conceptual framework is Young, Klosko, and Weishaar's five-group needs model and five-domain schema map.

**Status:** completed **17 October 2026 / ۱۷ اکتبر ۲۰۲۶**. Source verification, integrated HTML coverage, bilingual review, forty browser acceptance checks, local links, and print checks are complete. Both generated test PDFs preserved all eighty lines of a long worksheet response. The [evidence record](../research/abandonment-instability-sources.md) contains passage locators, study limitations, and the publication checklist. This is editorial guidance, not independent clinical validation.

All characters, inner statements, dialogue, bodily cues, recognition prompts, and completed worksheet entries are original teaching illustrations. They are not published cases, diagnostic criteria, questionnaire items, or tested interventions. References **1–6** are consulted sources; reference **7** is further reading with publisher metadata checked, not reviewed clinical chapters.

**Clinical emphasis:** the central concern is continuity and reliability of important connection. Closeness, reassurance, grief, needing practical help, and reacting to actual broken commitments are not themselves evidence of a schema. Repeated insecurity can deserve attention without assuming that the person's perception is wrong or that another person's harmful behavior was caused by their fear.

## 2 Understanding this schema / شناخت این طرحواره

### Schema at a glance / طرحواره در یک نگاه

| Required item | Content to preserve in both languages |
| --- | --- |
| Brief definition | An enduring expectation that important people will become unavailable or unreliable can make ordinary gaps in contact feel like the beginning of a lasting loss. The anticipated loss may involve leaving, inconsistent presence, or death; it is the feared instability of connection, not one particular behavior, that unites the formulation. [1](#ref-1) [2](#ref-2) |
| Domain | **Disconnection & Rejection / بریدگی و طرد**; link to [the overview's domain entry](../../Guide%20to%20Schema%20Domains%20and%20Recognition.html#domain-disconnection). This broad domain groups expectations that dependable care, safety, belonging, or acceptance will not be available. A domain is a conceptual grouping, not a diagnosis or known personal cause. [1](#ref-1) [2](#ref-2) |
| Relevant core needs | Secure attachment, including safety, stability, nurturance, and acceptance, is central. Autonomy/competence and freedom to express valid needs/emotions can also matter in a particular situation. Link to the dedicated discussion in section 3. [1](#ref-1) [4](#ref-4) |
| Original possible inner message | “People I rely on will not stay available; a gap means I am about to lose them.” / «کسانی که به آن‌ها تکیه می‌کنم در دسترس نمی‌مانند؛ فاصله یعنی در آستانهٔ از دست دادنشان هستم.» |

Introduce fictional **Mina / مینا** and her usually reliable friend **Leila / لیلا**. Leila is working and has not answered a nonurgent afternoon message. The observable fact is a delayed reply; Mina's immediate conclusion that the friendship is ending is an interpretation. The story returns in sections 6–8 and the completed worksheets.

Separate the pattern from an actual breakup, bereavement, repeated unreliability, understandable reassurance after a rupture, and practical dependence caused by access needs or circumstances. A relationship may be unstable in reality. Examining a schema should help someone assess the situation, not teach them to disregard evidence or tolerate mistreatment.

## 3 Core emotional needs and healthy alternatives / نیازهای هیجانی بنیادین و پاسخ‌های سالم

### Which core emotional needs may be unmet or frustrated? / کدام نیازهای هیجانی بنیادین ممکن است برآورده نشده باشند؟

Name Young et al.'s five broad groupings: secure attachment; autonomy, competence, and identity; freedom to express valid needs and emotions; spontaneity and play; realistic limits and self-control. Stability, nurturance, and acceptance are aspects of the first grouping, not extra categories. “Unmet” or “frustrated” describes insufficient support for a legitimate need; it does not describe a damaged person. This is a conceptual framework and an individual formulation remains open to revision. [1](#ref-1) [4](#ref-4)

| Need and relevance | Everyday meaning and possible frustration | Original fictional support example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Secure attachment: safety and stability — central | Connection has enough predictability and repair to remain usable across everyday absences. Repeated unexplained disappearance or unpredictable care may frustrate this need. Stability does not mean continuous access or a guarantee that nobody will ever leave. | Mina hears that Leila will be offline at work and they agree to catch up later. “Unavailable now” can coexist with “the friendship continues.” If the plan changes, Leila explains and they renegotiate. |
| Secure attachment: nurturance and acceptance — central components | A person's distress and wish for connection can receive care without ridicule or the demand to be constantly easygoing. Dismissal of sadness can leave the person feeling that needing others is unacceptable. | Mina says the uncertainty was hard. Leila acknowledges the feeling and remains clear that repeated calls interrupt work. Warmth and a limit can occur together. |
| Autonomy, competence, and identity — context dependent | A person can keep meaningful activities and make choices during an absence while receiving necessary help. This need may be insufficiently supported if independent action is discouraged or a person's options are genuinely restricted. | Mina resumes preparing dinner while waiting. Someone who needs assistance arranges reliable backup support; accepting disability-related or practical help does not defeat autonomy. |
| Freedom to express valid needs and emotions — context dependent | A person can name a wish for contact and discuss feasible arrangements rather than hide it or demand a guarantee. A need may be frustrated when requests are mocked or cannot be discussed. | Mina asks whether an evening check-in is workable. Leila can agree, offer another time, or explain a limit. The need remains valid even if one proposed arrangement is unavailable. |

Complete fictional need sequence: an unexpectedly delayed answer → Mina thinks the friendship is disappearing → she feels afraid and wants dependable connection → she checks what is known, names the wish, and negotiates realistic contact. The outcome may be a later conversation with some anxiety remaining. The supported need is continuity and responsiveness; an instant answer is only one possible strategy, not the need itself.

### Healthy connection and rigid certainty-seeking / ارتباط سالم و جست‌وجوی خشکِ قطعیت

Compare flexible trust with requiring certainty: looking at a relationship's pattern over time; being able to ask for comfort; accepting ordinary separate activity; having a range of supports; responding to real broken commitments; and making repairs after a strained exchange. Neither compulsory independence nor unlimited availability is the goal. Cultural closeness, caregiving, and practical obligations can shape appropriate contact arrangements.

## 4 Recognizing the pattern / شناخت نشانه‌های الگو

### Visible signs / نشانه‌های آشکار

Possible outward patterns include repeated demands for an immediate reply, escalating conflict around ordinary separations, abandoning one's plans to monitor contact, or accepting chronically uncertain connection as inevitable. Show the distinction between event and meaning: “There has been no answer this afternoon” versus “I have been replaced.” These actions only become informative when their function, context, rigidity, and costs are explored. [1](#ref-1) [4](#ref-4)

### Hidden or masked signs / نشانه‌های پنهان یا پوشیده

Fictional contrast: Mina says she prefers not to depend on anyone and appears composed; privately she is frightened by closeness and stops initiating contact to avoid another anticipated loss. The apparent self-sufficiency may protect against painful vulnerability while limiting support. Another person might appear agreeable while quietly accepting repeated broken promises because dependable care seems unavailable. Neither privacy, calmness, nor independence establishes an underlying fear. These are teaching distinctions, not validated symptom categories.

### Common triggers and early cues / محرک‌های رایج و نشانه‌های اولیه

Potential triggers include delayed replies, a changed contact routine, a loved one's travel, an ordinary disagreement, or an upcoming ending. Possible early cues are a sinking stomach, tight chest, restless checking, grief, fear, or a flash of anger; a possible thought is “This is the beginning of losing them.” Bodily cues are nonspecific. A new loss, actual neglect, unsafe behavior, work demands, illness, communication barriers, or an unmet practical obligation may better explain a particular reaction. Readers may conclude that the pattern does not fit.

## 5 Possible origins and unmet needs / ریشه‌های احتمالی و نیازهای برآورده‌نشده

Describe possible pathways involving repeated unpredictability in important care, disrupted contact, or frightening losses alongside differences in temperament and available support. Tie these possibilities to the needs for stability, care, and acceptance already explained in section 3. Separation or divorce alone does not establish a schema, an inevitable outcome, or blame. Different adults can respond differently to superficially similar histories. [1](#ref-1) [4](#ref-4)

The direct observational study considered reported parental separation/divorce, abandonment expectations, and adult attachment. Its statistical associations do not show that a particular event caused a particular person's schema, nor that the tested statistical pathway is the actual developmental sequence. [5](#ref-5) A reader does not need to identify an origin to examine a present pattern. Do not invite memory recovery or supply unreported histories for the fictional characters.

## 6 How the cycle continues / چرخه چگونه ادامه پیدا می‌کند

Use six expandable steps with essential labels visible. The following is one **fictional example / مثال فرضی**, not a prediction about every reader. Coping classification is a formulation of function. [1](#ref-1) [3](#ref-3) [4](#ref-4)

| Step | Mina's original sequence |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | Leila has not replied to a nonurgent afternoon message while working; no urgent responsibility or agreed deadline has been missed. |
| Meaning or rule | “She is pulling away. I must make her prove she is staying.” Mina feels fear and a sinking stomach; Vulnerable Child is one possible mode description. |
| Coping response | Hurt shifts into angry urgency. Mina sends repeated demands that Leila stop work and confirm the friendship immediately, trying to force certainty and control availability. In this formulation, this is overcompensation; Angry Child may shift toward Overcompensator. |
| Immediate effect | Sending another demand briefly feels active and powerful. Uncertainty remains and the urge returns. |
| Later cost | Mina loses her afternoon activity. Leila later explains that work was interrupted and asks for a limit on nonurgent calls. Mina feels shame and tells herself she deserves to be left; Punitive Parent may become active. Leila's boundary is not proof the relationship has ended. |
| Reinforcement | Mina interprets the strained exchange as confirmation that connection is always precarious. Renewed vigilance makes the next ordinary delay feel more threatening. |

**Specific Healthy Adult interruption:** at the first checking urge, Mina names fear without treating it as proof, checks the known work schedule and previous reliability, makes one proportionate request for later contact if needed, and resumes a chosen activity. If the pattern of behavior is actually unreliable, she gathers that information and considers support and boundaries. A later reply and a work boundary can coexist; neither guarantees permanent security nor proves rejection.

**Alternative branch:** Mina might instead silence the conversation and cancel a future meeting to stop feeling exposed. Avoidance/Detached Protector offers immediate distance but blocks a chance for connection or clarification. Taking a planned short break can be healthy; the function and longer pattern distinguish it from rigid withdrawal.

## 7 Coping styles and modes / سبک‌های مقابله‌ای و ذهنیت‌ها

### How the concepts relate / ارتباط مفهوم‌ها

A **schema / طرحواره** is the recurring meaning or expectation; a **coping style / سبک مقابله‌ای** is a broad way of responding to it; a **coping response / واکنش مقابله‌ای** is what happens on this occasion; a **mode / ذهنیت** describes the state active now. Modes can shift and overlap. They are not diagnoses, fixed identities, or evidence of literal child/parent parts. [1](#ref-1) [3](#ref-3) [4](#ref-4)

### Three coping styles / سه سبک مقابله‌ای

| Style | Schema-specific function and fictional action | Immediate function, later cost, and flexible alternative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surrender / تسلیم | A person treats unreliable connection as all they can expect and keeps accepting repeated unexplained disappearances without examining options. | The relationship remains familiar and there is no immediate confrontation; dependable support remains unavailable. Consider the pattern, express a reasonable need, and seek support for realistic choices. Constraints may limit options; remaining in a relationship alone does not establish surrender. |
| Avoidance / اجتناب | Mina withdraws from closeness or declines a meeting to avoid the fear that connection will be lost. | Less exposure to fear now; less opportunity for support, clarification, or repair later. A limited pause followed by a manageable conversation may allow both protection and connection. |
| Overcompensation / جبران افراطی | Mina excessively tries to control another person's availability so separation cannot happen, demanding immediate proof of commitment. | A brief sense of control; interruption, conflict, and increased vigilance may follow. Name the feeling, stop the coercive demand, and negotiate feasible contact while respecting both people's limits. |

Do not classify reassurance-seeking by appearance. Brockman et al. also describe repeated checking as surrender when it follows the abandonment belief; Young et al.'s coping table illustrates excessive clinging and attacks around separation under overcompensation. These are function-dependent formulations, not contradictory diagnostic rules. Ordinary reassurance and a clear contact request can be healthy. [1](#ref-1) [4](#ref-4)

### Modes that may become active / ذهنیت‌هایی که ممکن است فعال شوند

| Mode | Possible cue and original inner sentence | Feeling/action, need, and Healthy Adult response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vulnerable Child / کودک آسیب‌پذیر | Delayed contact: “I am about to be left alone.” | Fear, grief, and a sinking stomach. Acknowledge the need for reliable care, check facts, and offer comfort without asserting that the feared loss is certain. |
| Angry Child / کودک خشمگین | Hurt becomes protest: “You should be here when I need you!” | Heat and an urge to shout. Take the wish seriously while limiting attacks; turn the protest into a clear, feasible request. Anger alone does not establish this mode. |
| Detached Protector / محافظ جداشده | Closeness feels exposing: “I will stop needing anyone.” | Numbness, distraction, or cutting off a conversation to escape vulnerability. Allow a manageable pause and reconnect with one feeling or support rather than requiring total withdrawal. |
| Overcompensator / جبران‌گر افراطی | Uncertainty feels intolerable: “I will make sure you cannot pull away.” | Tight urgency and controlling demands. Stop pressure, recognize its effect, and discuss a contact arrangement that respects both people. |
| Punitive Parent / والد تنبیه‌گر | A boundary or conflict occurs: “You are too needy; you deserve to be left.” | Shame, harsh blame, and denying oneself care. Reject humiliation, separate the person from a specific behavior, and make a proportionate repair. |
| Healthy Adult / بزرگسال سالم | The first checking urge creates a pause: “This fear matters; a delay alone does not tell me what is happening.” | Check evidence, allow feelings, ask clearly, continue an activity, and use support or boundaries if warranted. Care and responsibility coexist; anxiety may remain. |

Use the project's glossary consistently and cite the original mode listing. The table's cue sentences and bodily experiences are original, nonspecific illustrations; not every mode needs to occur. [3](#ref-3)

### Recurring sequence and alternative branch / توالی تکرارشونده و مسیر جایگزین

Revisit Mina's same event: delayed reply → Vulnerable Child fear → Angry Child protest → Overcompensator demands → friend's work boundary → Punitive Parent blame → renewed vulnerability at the next delay. Alternatively fear → Detached Protector withdrawal → short relief but reduced connection. Healthy Adult can enter at the first urge or later, acknowledge impact, repair an angry message, and discuss a future plan. Missing an early pause does not make repair impossible.

## 8 Everyday examples / مثال‌های روزمره

- **Friendship, complete worked example:** use Mina's situation, meaning, feelings/body cues, function-based coping, immediate relief, later cost, need, and alternative response from sections 6–7. In a repair conversation she says, “I was frightened and kept interrupting you. I am sorry for the repeated demands. Can we find a realistic time to catch up?” Leila cares about Mina and also needs uninterrupted work. They agree a later time; Mina still feels some unease. No reply is treated as proof of permanent safety.
- **Partnership:** a partner plans an evening with friends. An ordinary separate activity is interpreted as replacement; repeated tests of love can strain closeness. The pair discuss what contact is reasonable and keep their separate plans. Support is not a promise of uninterrupted access, and wanting closeness is not a problem in itself.
- **Family:** a relative who provides practical help will be away. The person both fears loss and genuinely needs assistance. A backup plan addresses the practical need; discussing the emotional fear may help too. Do not relabel unmet access or care arrangements as irrational dependence.
- **Genuinely unreliable relationship:** a partner repeatedly disappears after making commitments and refuses discussion or repair. The facts warrant attention. A person can seek trusted or professional support, clarify limits, and assess available options; the task is not to keep tolerating uncertainty to prove recovery. The partner remains responsible for their conduct.

Label every scenario fictional. Show effects on closeness, trust, receiving help, boundaries, and conflict without assuming other people's motives or making the reader responsible for mistreatment. A healthy reassurance request after a genuine rupture is a counterexample to automatic schema labeling.

## 9 Related patterns / الگوهای مرتبط

Use the same observable event: **an important person has not answered a message**. Different possible meanings can produce similar distress or checking. Conceptual distinctions do not choose a diagnosis and multiple patterns may be relevant. [1](#ref-1) [2](#ref-2)

| Pattern | Original possible meaning of the same event | Distinguishing emphasis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Abandonment / Instability — رهاشدگی / بی‌ثباتی | “This connection is disappearing.” | Continuity and reliability of the relationship. |
| Emotional Deprivation — محرومیت هیجانی | “Even if they remain, my feelings will not receive care.” | Quality of emotional response, not primarily whether the person will stay. |
| Mistrust / Abuse — بی‌اعتمادی / بدرفتاری | “They are deliberately using silence to hurt or control me.” | Anticipated exploitation or harm; check actual evidence rather than assume intent. |
| Defectiveness / Shame — نقص / شرم | “They have discovered something unacceptable about me.” | Perceived personal unworthiness or defect. Link to the [available detailed guide](../../Interactive%20Guide%20to%20the%20Defectiveness%20Shame%20Schema.html). |
| Dependence / Incompetence — وابستگی / بی‌کفایتی | “Without their help I cannot handle the task.” | Perceived inability to manage responsibilities; distinguish this from a real need for assistance. |

Link other schema names to their existing overview entries rather than invent detailed guides. The relation between emotional themes is conceptual, not evidence that one schema causes another.

## 10 Examining your experience / بررسی تجربهٔ خود

Label **Original reflection exercise / تمرین تأملی تألیفی**. Offer optional, unscored prompts about how a gap in contact is interpreted, what happens in the body, what the response seeks to achieve, and whether actual reliability or practical needs change the interpretation. A reader can skip any field, use everyday language, or write “not sure.”

Use the standard eight reflection fields and retain their optional mode/function helpers. The completed fictional example is:

| Shared field | Mina's completed entry |
| --- | --- |
| What happened, and what triggered the reaction? | Leila had not answered my nonurgent afternoon message while working. No agreed deadline was missed. |
| What did the situation seem to mean? What rule appeared? | I thought the friendship was ending and that I had to make her prove it was secure immediately. |
| What feelings and bodily sensations did I notice? | Fear and a sinking stomach, then anger and tension; later shame. I might call these Vulnerable Child, Angry Child, and Punitive Parent states. |
| What did I do, and what was that response trying to achieve? | I sent repeated demands to control when she answered and remove uncertainty; overcompensation is one possible formulation of this function. |
| What happened immediately, and what were the later costs? | Brief control, then more checking. I lost my afternoon activity and interrupted her work. Her boundary led to punitive self-blame and renewed vigilance. |
| What did I need in that situation? | Dependable connection, comfort, and a realistic understanding of availability. |
| What alternative could respect my needs and the situation? | Check the facts, acknowledge fear, make one feasible request for later contact, and resume dinner preparation. |
| What happened next, or what support would help? | Leila replied later and wanted fewer work interruptions. We arranged a conversation; anxiety remained. Next time the first urge to send another demand can remind me to pause. |

The eight fields have one shared set of controls, bilingual labels, print mirrors, and an independent clear button. Do not score answers, auto-label modes, or present this example as the required interpretation of someone else's experience.

## 11 Practicing change / تمرین تغییر

Use one small, original experiment in a sufficiently safe and generally reliable relationship. Separate facts from predictions; acknowledge the wish for contact; express one proportionate contact request or agree a check-in; return to a chosen activity; review what happened without requiring reassurance, perfect calm, or a particular reply. This is an educational application of the model, not a validated treatment protocol. [1](#ref-1) [4](#ref-4)

Do not turn “one request” into an inflexible rule. New practical information, a missed care obligation, an emergency, actual danger, or repeated broken commitments may justify a different response. Do not deliberately withhold essential contact or undertake an experiment in a coercive relationship. The practice is not a test of whether someone else loves the reader.

| Shared experiment field | Completed fictional plan and outcome |
| --- | --- |
| Situation and limits | A nonurgent afternoon message to Leila during work. The relationship is usually reliable and no essential task depends on an immediate answer. |
| Prediction | “If I do not keep pressing, she will forget me.” I expect fear, anger, and an urge to demand proof or withdraw. |
| Small action | Check the work context, make one clear request to talk later if needed, and spend a manageable period preparing dinner before reviewing the situation. |
| Support or stopping conditions | Use a reminder that fear is information, not certainty. Contact a trusted support if overwhelmed. Stop the experiment if an actual urgent responsibility or safety concern appears; seek help rather than forcing distress tolerance. |
| Observed outcome | Leila replied later and said repeated calls were difficult at work. We agreed a possible evening conversation. I finished some preparation but still checked once and felt anxious. |
| Learning and next adjustment | An ordinary delay and a boundary did not automatically mean the friendship had ended. I can notice the first demand sooner, acknowledge the interruption, and clarify future contact. One trial cannot guarantee the next outcome. |

Use six shared controls, with an independent clear button. An incomplete experiment or an unwelcome answer can still provide information about needs, cues, or relationship limits. No result proves a global negative belief about the person.

## 12 How therapy can help / درمان چگونه می‌تواند کمک کند

A clinician and client can examine recurring situations, current relationships and safety, needs, coping, modes, and alternative explanations. Relevant work can include evaluating predictions against evidence, practicing proportionate requests and boundaries, reviewing consequences, and building a more caring response to fear and self-blame. Adapt the pace collaboratively. [1](#ref-1) [4](#ref-4)

Explain the therapeutic relationship as a place to discuss reliability, contact expectations, missed connections, breaks, and eventual endings within explicit professional boundaries. Limited reparenting means a bounded therapeutic effort to respond to needs; it does not promise permanent availability or replace every outside support. Therapist-supported imagery and dialogues may explore painful meanings and rehearse supportive responses. Provide an overview, not independent instructions to relive difficult memories. The reviewed 2003 excerpt supports imagery/dialogues; do not attribute an unreviewed chair-work protocol to it. [1](#ref-1)

### When professional support may help / چه زمانی کمک حرفه‌ای می‌تواند مفید باشد؟

Suggest collaborative support when repeated fear, checking, conflict, withdrawal, or self-blame disrupts sleep, everyday activity, or relationships, or when reflection becomes overwhelming. Grief, an actual unstable relationship, other emotional difficulties, and practical support needs may require a different or additional formulation. No checklist score or duration cutoff is provided. The whole-treatment research in section 14 concerns a specified clinical population and does not imply that recognizing this schema means having that diagnosis.

## 13 Reviewing progress / مرور پیشرفت

Concrete progress may include noticing the first checking urge sooner, separating a delay from a prediction, tolerating some feeling while continuing a chosen activity, asking clearly, receiving support, recognizing real unreliability, and repairing pressure or an angry exchange. Different contact needs can be negotiated without either person surrendering all limits.

Do not set goals of never feeling afraid, never seeking reassurance, handling all needs alone, keeping every relationship, or obtaining a guarantee that nobody will leave. After a setback, identify the cue, assess any harm, make a specific repair where needed, and choose a smaller next step. “I needed connection and used pressure; I can take responsibility without deciding I deserve abandonment” is an original compassionate alternative.

## 14 What research supports / پژوهش‌ها از چه چیزی حمایت می‌کنند

- **Conceptual explanation:** the definition, five-group needs framework, coping functions, and mode vocabulary organize a possible formulation. They are not proof of the reader's history or diagnosis. Young's official excerpt, ISST, the original mode listing, and the 2023 conceptual chapter are clinical sources. [1](#ref-1) [2](#ref-2) [3](#ref-3) [4](#ref-4)
- **Schema-specific observational evidence:** D'Rozario and Pilkington studied 426 adults using online measures. A reported history of parental separation/divorce was associated with anxious and avoidant attachment, with abandonment expectations involved in the statistical mediation model. Only the abstract and bibliographic metadata were consulted. The associations do not establish developmental causation or treatment benefit. [5](#ref-5)
- **Whole-treatment evidence:** Arntz et al. randomized 495 adults with borderline personality disorder; 494 were analyzed after one withdrawal of consent. Combined individual and group Schema Therapy reduced BPD severity more than optimal usual treatment and predominantly group Schema Therapy. Predominantly group treatment did not significantly outperform usual treatment on the primary outcome. Attrition, differences in usual treatment, and therapist experience limit interpretation. This is evidence for a complete treatment in that population, not the isolated Abandonment schema, this guide, or its exercises. [6](#ref-6)
- **Practical guidance and remaining uncertainty:** this guide's prompts, conversations, and practices are original educational applications. They were not tested in those studies. A recognizable example is not a diagnosis, a treatment response prediction, or validation of the Persian translation. Further reading is separated below from clinical material actually consulted.
