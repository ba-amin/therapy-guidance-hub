# Subjugation guide draft

Authoring draft for **Subjugation / اطاعت**, aligned with the [shared standard](../schema-guide-standard.md) and [reusable template](../schema-guide-template.md). Expand into equivalent English/Persian content. Examples and prompts are original, not assessment items.

## 1 Getting started

Offer the shared recognition, examination, and practice paths. Present this as exploration, not a diagnosis.

**Sample opening — EN:** “Do you sometimes agree before checking what you want? This guide invites you to explore whether fear is narrowing your choices.”

**نمونهٔ آغاز — FA:** «آیا گاهی پیش از اینکه ببینید خودتان چه می‌خواهید، موافقت می‌کنید؟ این راهنما فرصتی است برای بررسی اینکه آیا ترس، انتخاب‌های شما را محدود می‌کند.»

## 2 Understanding this schema

Define subjugation as suppressing preferences or feelings because resisting seems likely to provoke anger, retaliation, or abandonment. Distinguish the needs and emotions forms. [1](#ref-1)

**Original core rule:** “If I disagree, something bad will happen to our relationship.” / «اگر مخالفت کنم، اتفاق بدی برای رابطه‌مان می‌افتد.»

## 3 Healthy needs and flexible alternatives

Compare chosen cooperation with agreement driven by fear. Use a table covering freedom to decline, awareness of preferences, negotiation, and later feelings. Include generosity and interdependence as legitimate choices. The alternative is room for both people's needs, rather than winning every disagreement.

## 4 Recognizing the pattern

Use optional examples: an automatic “whatever you want,” holding one's breath, postponing requests, or feeling resentful afterward. Invite readers to identify context and frequency without treating examples as proof. Ask whether the pattern appears with everyone or particular people.

## 5 Possible origins and unmet needs

Invite exploration of whether expressing preferences once felt unwelcome or costly. Offer hypotheses, not conclusions about caregivers. Include present circumstances: unequal power or actual threats may restrict choices. Never reinterpret real coercion as merely a mistaken belief. Do not require childhood recollection.

## 6 How the cycle continues

Build six expandable steps around this **fictional illustration**:

1. A friend proposes an inconvenient meeting time.
2. Leila predicts disagreement will upset the friend and notices tension.
3. She hides her preference and agrees immediately.
4. Uncertainty briefly decreases.
5. She misses needed rest.
6. With no opportunity to test negotiation, the belief that agreement is necessary remains unchallenged.

Invite an alternative at each step; do not present this sequence as universal.

## 7 Coping styles and modes

Classify by function: surrender gives up choice; avoidance evades disagreement; overcompensation may become rigid domination. Healthy assertion is different. [1](#ref-1)

Use illustrative voices: a frightened part, an appeasing response, and a Healthy Adult checking safety and preference. Mode labels describe changing states, not identities. Do not equate “freeze” or “fawn” with a clinical diagnosis.

## 8 Everyday examples

**Worked fictional example:** A trusted friend requests help on Saturday. Leila wants a quiet morning but says yes immediately. Her feared outcome is rejection; the cost is losing recovery time. A flexible response is: “I can help for an hour in the afternoon.” The friend might accept or decline; success means making a considered choice.

Add examples involving work deadlines, family plans, household tasks, and restaurants. Vary outcomes and power differences.

## 9 Related patterns

Replace the existing “Differential Diagnosis” heading. Compare questions: “Am I preventing retaliation, seeking praise, avoiding guilt, or expecting rejection because I feel unworthy?” Explain that multiple concerns can coexist. Avoid diagnosing personality disorders from agreement, conflict avoidance, or a schema label.

## 10 Examining your experience

Replace severity percentages and thresholds with unscored reflection. Use the shared eight-field worksheet: situation, meaning, feelings, response and function, immediate and later consequences, needs, alternative, and outcome or support.

**Original prompt:** “Before answering, what did I want—and what did I predict would happen if I said it?” Allow “I am not sure.”

## 11 Practicing change

**Original low-stakes experiment:** With a person who has previously respected differences, express a small preference about a film or meal. Record the predicted response, actual response, and what remains uncertain. A pause—“Let me check and reply”—can be the whole experiment. Where disagreement risks retaliation, prioritize support and safety over confrontation.

## 12 How therapy can help

Suggest therapy topics: distinguishing current danger from anticipated rejection, identifying preferences, and choosing realistic boundaries. Explain collaborative assessment and therapist-supported experiential work with verified citations before publication. Therapy should leave room to disagree; worksheets are not a compliance test.

## 13 Reviewing progress

Track noticing preferences sooner, pausing before agreement, communicating safely, and recovering after disagreement. Include voluntary agreement as a possible healthy outcome. Avoid streaks, obedience to exercises, or a requirement to say no regularly. Ask what became more freely chosen.

## 14 What research supports

Present the conceptual account separately from empirical evidence. **Research still required:** extract any schema-specific study's population, measures, design, findings, and limitations before adding claims. Do not imply a general schema-therapy trial establishes this guide's effectiveness. This draft includes no validated screening or treatment instrument.
