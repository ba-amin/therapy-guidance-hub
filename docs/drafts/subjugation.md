# Subjugation guide draft

Authoring draft for **Subjugation / اطاعت**, aligned with the [shared standard](../schema-guide-standard.md) and [reusable template](../schema-guide-template.md). Expand into equivalent English/Persian content. Examples and prompts are original, not assessment items.

## 1 Getting started

Offer the shared recognition, examination, and practice paths. Present this as exploration, not a diagnosis.

**Sample opening — EN:** “Do you sometimes agree before checking what you want? This guide invites you to explore whether fear is narrowing your choices.”

**نمونهٔ آغاز — FA:** «آیا گاهی پیش از اینکه ببینید خودتان چه می‌خواهید، موافقت می‌کنید؟ این راهنما فرصتی است برای بررسی اینکه آیا ترس، انتخاب‌های شما را محدود می‌کند.»

## 2 Understanding this schema

Define subjugation as suppressing preferences or feelings because resisting seems likely to provoke anger, retaliation, or abandonment. Distinguish the needs and emotions forms. [1](#ref-1)

**Original core rule:** “If I disagree, something bad will happen to our relationship.” / «اگر مخالفت کنم، اتفاق بدی برای رابطه‌مان می‌افتد.»

## 3 Healthy needs and flexible alternatives

Compare chosen cooperation with agreement driven by fear. Use a table covering freedom to decline, awareness of preferences, negotiation, and later feelings. Include generosity and interdependence as legitimate choices. The alternative is room for both people's needs, rather than winning every disagreement.

## 4 Recognizing the pattern

Use optional examples: an automatic “whatever you want,” holding one's breath, postponing requests, or feeling resentful afterward. Invite readers to identify context and frequency without treating examples as proof. Ask whether the pattern appears with everyone or particular people.

## 5 Possible origins and unmet needs

Invite exploration of whether expressing preferences once felt unwelcome or costly. Offer hypotheses, not conclusions about caregivers. Include present circumstances: unequal power or actual threats may restrict choices. Never reinterpret real coercion as merely a mistaken belief. Do not require childhood recollection.

## 6 How the cycle continues

Build six expandable steps around this **fictional illustration**:

1. A friend proposes an inconvenient meeting time.
2. Leila predicts disagreement will upset the friend and notices tension.
3. She hides her preference and agrees immediately.
4. Uncertainty briefly decreases.
5. She misses needed rest.
6. With no opportunity to test negotiation, the belief that agreement is necessary remains unchallenged.

Invite an alternative at each step; do not present this sequence as universal. At steps 2–3, distinguish the feared meaning from the response used to cope with it. Link to the possible mode changes in section 7: a frightened state may be followed by appeasing, withdrawal, or controlling. Ask where the reader first notices a shift; no particular sequence or label is required.

## 7 Coping styles and modes

### Separate the three ideas

A **schema / طرحواره** is the recurring meaning: “My preferences put this relationship at risk.” A **coping style / سبک مقابله‌ای** is a broad way of responding to that meaning; a **coping response / پاسخ مقابله‌ای** is the specific action or strategy used in one situation. A **mode / ذهنیت** describes their current emotional and coping state. One schema can involve different responses and changing modes. [1](#ref-1)

### Three possible coping functions

The following are **original fictional illustrations / نمونه‌های فرضی تألیفی**, informed by the coping framework. Classify the response by its function in this situation, rather than by its appearance. [1](#ref-1)

| Coping style | Trigger and feared meaning | Possible response | Immediate function and possible later cost | A flexible alternative |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Surrender / تسلیم** | A friend proposes a time; “My preference must give way or they will get angry.” | Leila agrees before checking her availability, treating the rule as binding. | Avoids the anticipated conflict in the moment; her preference remains unknown and agreement can feel increasingly compulsory. | With this respectful friend, pause to check availability and offer one workable time: “Afternoon works better for me.” |
| **Avoidance / اجتناب** | A group begins arranging a trip; “Any discussion will trap me in a disagreement.” | Leila stops opening the planning messages to avoid the discussion and the feelings it brings. | Reduces exposure to the feared conflict; decisions happen without her and the possibility of safe negotiation remains unexplored. | Read one message when ready and communicate a limited choice: “I can join for one day; I need more time to decide which.” |
| **Overcompensation / جبران افراطی** | Someone suggests a different plan; “If I let anyone influence me, I will lose all control.” | Leila insists that every detail must be her decision and dismisses others' preferences. | Creates a brief sense of protection through control; reciprocal choice becomes difficult and conflict can appear to confirm that negotiation is dangerous. | State one genuine limit and invite a shared decision about the rest: “I need to leave by six; which starting time suits us both?” |

Saying yes, taking time away, and saying no can each be considered, healthy choices. Actual coercion, limited options, and safety must be examined before calling a response maladaptive. Healthy assertion leaves room for both people's needs; it does not require dominating or automatically opposing others.

### Possible modes and how they might become noticeable

Mode names below use Young and First's terminology. [2](#ref-2) The activation cues, inner sentences, bodily sensations, and alternatives are **original fictional possibilities**, not diagnostic signs. A mode is a temporary state, not a fixed identity or a label assigned from one behavior. “Child” and “Parent” are model terms; they do not establish a childhood event or identify a particular caregiver. Do not infer a Child mode from anger, tears, or compliance alone. These modes are not exclusive to Subjugation; coping modes and coping styles are related concepts, not interchangeable labels.

| Possible mode | Example cue, inner speech, feeling/body, and action | Possible Healthy Adult response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Vulnerable Child / کودک آسیب‌پذیر** | A friend pauses after a request. “I am about to lose them.” Leila feels frightened, her stomach tightens, and she searches the friend's expression for danger. | “This fear needs care. What evidence do I have about safety now? I can take time before answering.” |
| **Compliant Surrenderer / تسلیم‌شوندهٔ مطیع** | Asked to change plans, “Just agree and keep the peace.” She feels tense and resigned, lowers her voice, and drops her preference. | “I can check what I want and what I can realistically offer.” |
| **Detached Protector / محافظ جداشده** | Another message arrives after an uncomfortable exchange. “I do not want to feel any of this.” She feels distant or blank and repeatedly distracts herself rather than noticing her needs. | “A pause may help. I can return when ready, or seek support if contact is unsafe.” |
| **Overcompensator / جبران‌گر افراطی** | Someone questions her plan. “Nobody gets a say over me.” With a rigid jaw and a surge of determination, she refuses all discussion to prevent feeling controlled. | “Having a boundary does not require controlling every decision. I can state one limit and hear the other person's preference.” |
| **Demanding Parent / والد پرتوقع** | She notices a wish that conflicts with someone else's. “You must always be agreeable.” Pressure and shoulder tension accompany rehearsing a perfect, unobjectionable reply. | “Consideration matters; constant agreement is not a fair requirement.” |
| **Punitive Parent / والد تنبیه‌گر** | After stating a preference, “You were selfish; you deserve to be ignored.” Shame and a sinking feeling lead her to attack herself or retract a reasonable request. | “I can examine my words and repair any harm without condemning myself for having needs.” |
| **Healthy Adult / بزرگسال سالم** | Before replying, “Both our preferences matter; I need to check the situation.” Some anxiety remains, but she pauses, checks safety and capacity, and makes a considered offer. | Support this state with time, clear information, and help where needed; it does not require feeling completely calm. |

### A repeated sequence and an interruption

**Fictional example — EN:** A trusted friend asks Leila to change Saturday's plans. Her stomach tightens: “If I disagree, I will lose the friendship” (**possible Vulnerable Child**). “You must be easy to get along with” adds pressure (**possible Demanding Parent**). She agrees immediately (**surrender; possible Compliant Surrenderer**) and feels brief relief. Later, missing her quiet morning, she feels resentful and stops reading messages to shut out the tension (**a switch to avoidance; possible Detached Protector**). Her friend, unaware of the unspoken preference, repeats a similar request the following week. Leila takes the repetition as proof that her wishes cannot matter. This is one possible loop, not the inevitable meaning of either person's behavior.

**An interruption — EN:** At the first tightening sensation, Leila pauses: “I need a moment to check my plans.” Her **Healthy Adult** response checks whether disagreement is safe, acknowledges her preference, and offers an afternoon meeting. Her friend may agree or be disappointed. Leila can learn about negotiation while respecting both people, rather than making a pleasant response the test of success.

**مثال فرضی — FA:** دوستی قابل‌اعتماد از لیلا می‌خواهد برنامهٔ شنبه را تغییر دهد. شکمش منقبض می‌شود: «اگر مخالفت کنم، دوستی‌مان را از دست می‌دهم» (**احتمالاً ذهنیت کودک آسیب‌پذیر**). «باید همیشه خوش‌برخورد و سازگار باشی» فشار را بیشتر می‌کند (**احتمالاً والد پرتوقع**). بی‌درنگ موافقت می‌کند (**تسلیم؛ احتمالاً تسلیم‌شوندهٔ مطیع**) و برای مدتی آسوده می‌شود. بعد، از دست‌دادن صبح آرامش او را دلخور می‌کند و برای دورشدن از تنش، پیام‌ها را نمی‌خواند (**تغییر به اجتناب؛ احتمالاً محافظ جداشده**). دوستش که از خواستهٔ بیان‌نشدهٔ او خبر ندارد، هفتهٔ بعد درخواست مشابهی می‌کند. لیلا این تکرار را نشانه‌ای می‌گیرد که خواسته‌هایش نمی‌تواند مهم باشد. این فقط یک چرخهٔ احتمالی است و معنای قطعی رفتار هیچ‌کدام نیست.

**نقطهٔ تغییر — FA:** لیلا با نخستین حس انقباض مکث می‌کند: «کمی وقت می‌خواهم تا برنامه‌ام را بررسی کنم.» پاسخ **بزرگسال سالم** او امن‌بودن مخالفت را بررسی می‌کند، خواسته‌اش را به رسمیت می‌شناسد و دیدار در بعدازظهر را پیشنهاد می‌دهد. دوستش ممکن است بپذیرد یا ناراحت شود. لیلا می‌تواند دربارهٔ گفت‌وگو و توجه به نیازهای هر دو نفر یاد بگیرد، بدون اینکه واکنش خوشایند دوستش معیار موفقیت باشد.

**Alternative branch — EN:** With the same request, Leila might instead dictate all future meeting times because she fears any compromise means losing control (**overcompensation; possible Overcompensator**). Compare this with a considered refusal of this one request: the function and flexibility distinguish them.

**مسیر احتمالی دیگر — FA:** در برابر همان درخواست، لیلا ممکن است زمان همهٔ دیدارهای بعدی را یک‌طرفه تعیین کند، چون می‌ترسد هر توافقی به معنای ازدست‌دادن کنترل باشد (**جبران افراطی؛ احتمالاً جبران‌گر افراطی**). این را با ردکردن سنجیدهٔ همین یک درخواست مقایسه کنید؛ کارکرد واکنش و میزان انعطاف، تفاوت را روشن می‌کند.

## 8 Everyday examples

**Worked fictional example:** A trusted friend requests help on Saturday. Leila wants a quiet morning but says yes immediately. Her feared outcome is rejection; the cost is losing recovery time. A flexible response is: “I can help for an hour in the afternoon.” The friend might accept or decline; success means making a considered choice.

Add examples involving work deadlines, family plans, household tasks, and restaurants. Vary outcomes and power differences. In each, identify the coping function and a possible current mode with evidence from the person's experience; allow “unclear.” Include one contrast showing that the same “yes” can be either fearful surrender or freely chosen help. Link at least one example back to section 7's sequence.

## 9 Related patterns

Replace the existing “Differential Diagnosis” heading. Compare questions: “Am I preventing retaliation, seeking praise, avoiding guilt, or expecting rejection because I feel unworthy?” Explain that multiple concerns can coexist. Avoid diagnosing personality disorders from agreement, conflict avoidance, or a schema label.

## 10 Examining your experience

Replace severity percentages and thresholds with unscored reflection. Use the shared eight-field worksheet: situation, meaning, feelings, response and function, immediate and later consequences, needs, alternative, and outcome or support.

**Original prompt:** “Before answering, what did I want—and what did I predict would happen if I said it?” Allow “I am not sure.”

Add optional cues within the existing fields, without adding a score: field 3, “What state did I notice first?”; field 4, “Was I giving in, escaping the feelings, or trying to prevent anyone influencing me—and did that change?”; field 5, “What briefly improved and what repeated?”; field 7, “What would a supportive, realistic response protect?” Use everyday descriptions when a mode name does not fit.

**FA prompt:** «ابتدا در چه حالتی بودم؟ بعد چه تغییری کرد؟ واکنشم قرار بود از چه چیزی جلوگیری کند، و چه چیزی دوباره تکرار شد؟»

## 11 Practicing change

**Original low-stakes experiment:** With a person who has previously respected differences, express a small preference about a film or meal. Record the predicted response, actual response, and what remains uncertain. A pause—“Let me check and reply”—can be the whole experiment. Where disagreement risks retaliation, prioritize support and safety over confrontation.

Connect the plan to section 7: note one early cue, the usual coping response it precedes, and a brief Healthy Adult sentence. For example, when the urge to agree immediately appears, check availability before answering. Review both the outside response and any internal shift, including pressure to apologize for having a preference. Do not turn naming the “right” mode into another demand to comply.

## 12 How therapy can help

Suggest therapy topics: distinguishing current danger from anticipated rejection, identifying preferences, and choosing realistic boundaries. Explain collaborative assessment and therapist-supported experiential work with verified citations before publication. Therapy should leave room to disagree; worksheets are not a compliance test.

## 13 Reviewing progress

Track noticing preferences sooner, pausing before agreement, communicating safely, and recovering after disagreement. Include voluntary agreement as a possible healthy outcome. Avoid streaks, obedience to exercises, or a requirement to say no regularly. Ask what became more freely chosen.

Review whether the repeated sequence is easier to recognize: “Did I notice fear before agreeing, notice withdrawal afterward, or offer myself support sooner?” Later recognition still provides information. More choice and less self-punishment matter more than eliminating a mode or always identifying it correctly.

## 14 What research supports

Present the conceptual account separately from empirical evidence. **Research still required:** extract any schema-specific study's population, measures, design, findings, and limitations before adding claims. Do not imply a general schema-therapy trial establishes this guide's effectiveness. This draft includes no validated screening or treatment instrument.
