## 2 Understanding this schema

Define a recurrent difficulty managing impulses or continuing meaningful action through frustration; a quieter presentation can center on avoiding discomfort. Place the schema in **Impaired Limits / محدودیت‌های مختل**. [1](#ref-1) This describes a possible pattern, not someone's worth.

Original illustrative message: **“If this feels uncomfortable, I cannot keep going.” / «اگر این کار ناراحت‌کننده باشد، نمی‌توانم ادامه بدهم.»** Do not substitute “lazy,” “spoiled,” or “weak-willed” for an explanation.

## 3 Healthy needs and flexible alternatives

Compare immediate urges, realistic persistence, supportive structure, accountability, and permission to rest. Include flexible alternatives: making a task smaller, asking for clarity, planning a real break, or renegotiating an unrealistic commitment. A useful routine is not overcompensation; resting when depleted is not evidence of avoidance. Show how a plan can support both freedom and responsibility.

## 4 Recognizing the pattern

Use optional observations about waiting, repetitive tasks, interruptions, irritation, restlessness, and the wish for immediate relief. Ask about occasions when follow-through was easier and what was different: interest, instructions, company, resources, sleep, or capacity. Bodily experiences are clues to examine, not schema-specific signs. Include a contrasting example in which a person stops because the workload is unreasonable or they genuinely need rest.

## 5 Possible origins and unmet needs

Explore opportunities to learn manageable limits, receive encouragement through frustration, and experience supported autonomy. Keep developmental explanations tentative and allow different histories. Use Young's named needs framework consistently with the current guide. [2](#ref-2) Do not assume permissive caregiving, prescribe memory retrieval, or treat present executive-function difficulty as proof of a childhood cause.

## 6 How the cycle continues

Retain Neda's fictional community-project update from the existing guide and link it directly to the fuller mode sequence in section 7. Keep the six shared steps visible:

1. **Trigger:** a nonurgent update begins with sorting repetitive notes.
2. **Meaning:** “I cannot stand this; I need to feel interested first.” Neda notices irritation and tight shoulders.
3. **Coping response:** she closes the notes and opens a feed. First ask whether the action enacts “I cannot persist,” blocks uncomfortable feelings, or serves another function; do not infer a style from scrolling alone.
4. **Immediate effect:** the task-related pressure briefly recedes.
5. **Later cost:** the work remains, a teammate asks again, and there is less room for a planned walk.
6. **Reinforcement:** “I never follow through” feels more convincing. A punitive reaction adds discomfort to the next attempt, increasing the appeal of escape in this fictional sequence.

Mark the transition from relief to self-attack as a possible **mode change**, then use section 7 to explain it. An interruption begins with checking capacity and needs before choosing a small step or a communicated break. The sequence is a teaching formulation, not a demonstrated causal pathway for every reader.

## 7 Coping styles and modes

### Distinguish what is being described

A **schema** is an enduring theme; a **coping style** describes the response's relationship to that theme; a **coping response** is a specific action or mental strategy; a **mode** describes what is active now, including feelings and coping. A mode may involve several schemas, and the same schema can be met in different ways at different times. [2](#ref-2) Labels are tentative descriptions to revise with the reader, not identities or diagnoses. “Child” and “Parent” are model terms; they do not establish immaturity or identify an actual caregiver. In Neda's story, “I cannot persist” is the theme, closing the notes is an action, surrender is one possible function, and the later self-attacking state may be described as Punitive Parent. A child or parent mode is not an additional coping style.

**EN:** “What did this response help me do or avoid in that moment? What changed just before I reacted differently?”

**FA:** «این پاسخ در آن لحظه کمک کرد چه کاری انجام بدهم یا از چه چیزی دور بمانم؟ درست پیش از آنکه واکنشم عوض شود، چه چیزی تغییر کرد؟»

### Show all three coping styles by function

Young's conceptual examples distinguish giving up during a task, avoiding responsibility, and excessive self-discipline. [2](#ref-2) The following situations and formulations are **fictional examples / مثال‌های فرضی**. They are possibilities, not three types of people.

| Possible style | Trigger and meaning in this example | Action and immediate function | Possible later cost and repetition | Flexible response to explore |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Surrender / تسلیم** | A chosen hobby involves a dull practice step: “If I am bored, continuing is beyond me.” | Stop at the first dull step; act as though the predicted inability is settled. The struggle ends for now. | The person has little chance to discover what becomes manageable with support; the next dull step meets the same conclusion. | Check capacity, then try one small part before deciding whether to stop or adapt the task. |
| **Avoidance / اجتناب** | A message asks for a project update: “Opening it will bring the discomfort back.” | Leave the message unopened and absorb attention elsewhere, keeping frustration and guilt out of awareness temporarily. | Uncertainty and coordination problems grow; the next reminder feels harder to approach. | Read one sentence with support, then send a realistic status update or negotiate timing. |
| **Overcompensation / جبران افراطی** | After abandoning a plan: “Any freedom will prove I cannot regulate myself.” | Enforce a minute-by-minute schedule with no leisure to prove the feared inability wrong; briefly feel in control. | A rigid schedule crowds out rest and relationships. When it becomes unworkable, another lapse seems to justify even tighter control. | Use a short plan with breaks, realistic limits, and a way to restart after interruption. |

The same postponement could enact this schema while avoiding shame related to another schema. A strict schedule could instead surrender to Unrelenting Standards. Identify the meaning and function before choosing a label; healthy planning and needed rest do not belong automatically in these rows.

### Make relevant modes recognizable

The terminology below follows Young and First's 2003 listing. That listing includes **Impulsive/Undisciplined Child**, **Detached Protector**, **Overcompensator**, **Punitive Parent**, **Demanding or Critical Parent**, **Vulnerable Child**, and **Healthy Adult**. It includes distracting self-soothing within Detached Protector rather than naming a separate Detached Self-Soother. [3](#ref-3)

Explain the selected terms briefly: **Impulsive/Undisciplined Child** follows immediate urges; **Detached Protector** disconnects from difficult feelings; **Vulnerable Child** experiences distress and unmet needs; **Punitive Parent** condemns or punishes; **Demanding Parent** imposes rigid expectations; **Overcompensator** counters a feared vulnerability excessively; **Healthy Adult** combines care with realistic limits and responsibility. [3](#ref-3)

The table contains **original fictional illustrations**, not criteria or a required set of modes. The feelings and bodily clues are nonspecific. Use only formulations that help explain this person's experience, and permit “not sure,” “does not fit,” or an ordinary-language name.

| Possible active mode | Activation and original inner speech | Possible feeling/body clue and action | Need and a possible Healthy Adult response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Impulsive/Undisciplined Child / کودک تکانشی / بی‌انضباط** | A repetitive step interrupts Neda's wish for stimulation: “I want something more interesting now.” | Irritation, shifting in the chair; close the notes immediately without considering the agreement. | A manageable limit and room for enjoyment: “The urge is here. I can choose one small step, then decide about a real break.” |
| **Detached Protector / محافظ جداشده** | A reminder brings uncomfortable feelings: “I don't want to feel any of this.” | Feeling distant or absorbed; keep scrolling without noticing the content or the passing time. | Contact with feelings at a tolerable pace and support: “I can pause the feed, name one feeling, and decide whether I need help or rest.” |
| **Vulnerable Child / کودک آسیب‌پذیر** | A teammate asks again: “I feel exposed and alone with this.” | Sadness, a sinking feeling; hesitate to ask for assistance. | Understanding and practical help: “This is hard. I can ask which part matters first.” Do not assume this state occurs in every case. |
| **Punitive Parent / والد تنبیه‌گر** | Neda notices the missed agreement: “You deserve to feel awful for letting this happen.” | Shame, heat in the face; repeatedly attack herself instead of addressing the delay. | Respectful accountability: “I can acknowledge the impact and repair the agreement without humiliating myself.” |
| **Demanding Parent / والد پرتوقع** | Rest conflicts with an internal rule: “A responsible person must always be working.” | Pressure, tense jaw; extend work after a reasonable stopping point because the rule feels compulsory. | Permission for proportionate expectations: “Responsibility includes sleep and a workable workload.” Here the rule is obeyed, not used to disprove the self-control fear. |
| **Overcompensator / جبران‌گر افراطی** | Neda fears another lapse: “I will prove I am in control by allowing no flexibility.” | Urgency, braced shoulders; impose rules on every minute and reject helpful adjustments. | Reliable, flexible structure: “One clear priority and planned breaks can support me; total control is not required.” This formulation concerns the counteracting function, not the schedule's appearance. |
| **Healthy Adult / بزرگسال سالم** | Neda notices the urge, the missed agreement, or the need for rest: “What is happening, what matters, and what is workable?” | Discomfort may remain; she checks capacity, communicates honestly, and chooses a small action or a realistic break. | Combine care, limits, choice, and repair. A calm feeling is not required before taking a constructive step. |

Do not present Demanding Parent and Overcompensator as interchangeable labels for strictness. The cited listing distinguishes internalized demands from compensatory function. [3](#ref-3) An involuntary attention difficulty or an appropriate response to exhaustion also needs consideration before any mode formulation.

### Show a repeated sequence and a possible interruption

**Fictional example — EN.** Each week Neda opens the project notes after work. On this occasion, she has enough capacity for a short task but finds the first step boring. “I need relief now” accompanies restless irritation; an **Impulsive/Undisciplined Child** formulation may fit. Closing the notes enacts “I cannot persist” (**surrender**), and the feed briefly feels easier. When her teammate asks for the update, her response changes: “You deserve to feel awful.” This is a possible shift to **Punitive Parent**. Shame and tense shoulders follow. Neda then stops attending to her feelings and scrolls without taking in the content; **Detached Protector** and **avoidance** may describe that later function. The task remains, “I never follow through” feels confirmed, and the next reminder arrives with more pressure. This is one possible repeated sequence, not a prescribed order of modes.

**مثال فرضی — FA.** ندا هر هفته پس از کار یادداشت‌های پروژه را باز می‌کند. در این موقعیت برای یک کار کوتاه توان کافی دارد، اما مرحلهٔ اول برایش کسل‌کننده است. همراه با کلافگی و بی‌قراری فکر می‌کند: «همین حالا آرامش می‌خواهم»؛ توصیف **کودک تکانشی / بی‌انضباط** شاید به این لحظه بخورد. با بستن یادداشت‌ها مطابق فکر «نمی‌توانم ادامه بدهم» عمل می‌کند (**تسلیم**) و دیدن مطالب شبکهٔ اجتماعی موقتاً آسان‌تر به نظر می‌رسد. وقتی هم‌گروهی‌اش پیگیر گزارش می‌شود، واکنش او عوض می‌شود: «سزایت این است که حال بدی داشته باشی.» این می‌تواند تغییری به **والد تنبیه‌گر** باشد. شرم و انقباض شانه‌ها از راه می‌رسند. سپس ندا از توجه به احساس‌هایش فاصله می‌گیرد و بدون دنبال‌کردن محتوا به مرور مطالب ادامه می‌دهد؛ **محافظ جداشده** و **اجتناب** شاید کارکرد این پاسخِ بعدی را توصیف کنند. کار باقی می‌ماند، فکر «هیچ‌وقت کارها را دنبال نمی‌کنم» برایش تأیید می‌شود و یادآوری بعدی فشار بیشتری به همراه دارد. این فقط یکی از توالی‌های احتمالیِ تکرارشونده است، نه ترتیب ثابت ذهنیت‌ها.

**Possible interruption — EN.** At the first urge or after noticing self-attack, Neda tries a **Healthy Adult** response: “I am frustrated, and the agreement matters. Let me check what I need.” If she is depleted, she can take a real break and communicate a revised plan. If she has capacity, she can sort one note with a clear stopping point. In this example she returns for a few minutes, still feels bored, completes only part of the update, and tells her teammate what remains. The result is mixed, but she has practiced returning without punishment. Repetition to observe next time: does the first urge, the self-attack, or the return happen differently?

**وقفهٔ احتمالی در چرخه — FA.** در اولین میل به کنارگذاشتن کار یا پس از متوجه‌شدن سرزنش خود، ندا پاسخ **بزرگسال سالم** را امتحان می‌کند: «کلافه‌ام و قولی که داده‌ام هم مهم است. ببینم به چه چیزی نیاز دارم.» اگر توانش تمام شده باشد، می‌تواند واقعاً استراحت کند و دربارهٔ برنامهٔ تازه خبر بدهد. اگر توان کافی داشته باشد، می‌تواند یک یادداشت را با نقطهٔ توقف روشن مرتب کند. در این مثال، چند دقیقه برمی‌گردد، هنوز حوصله‌اش سر می‌رود، فقط بخشی از گزارش را انجام می‌دهد و به هم‌گروهی‌اش می‌گوید چه چیزی باقی مانده است. نتیجه کامل نیست، اما بازگشتن بدون تنبیه را تمرین کرده است. دفعهٔ بعد می‌تواند ببیند اولین میل، سرزنش خود یا شیوهٔ بازگشت چه تفاوتی کرده است.

**Alternative branch from the same trigger — EN.** At the same boring first step, a different Neda example could continue: “I must prove I can control myself.” She forces herself through the whole evening without a break, then feels exhausted. If the purpose is to disprove the feared inability, this may illustrate **overcompensation / Overcompensator**, rather than the surrender-to-avoidance sequence above. If she is simply obeying “rest is never allowed,” examine a demanding rule and possible Unrelenting Standards instead. Neither interpretation follows from hours worked alone. A balanced alternative is to choose a limited task, protect rest, and revise the plan when capacity changes.

**شاخهٔ دیگر از همان محرک — FA.** در همان مرحلهٔ اولِ کسل‌کننده، مثال دیگری از ندا می‌تواند این‌گونه ادامه پیدا کند: «باید ثابت کنم می‌توانم خودم را کنترل کنم.» او تمام عصر بدون استراحت به خود فشار می‌آورد و بعد احساس فرسودگی می‌کند. اگر هدف، ردکردنِ ناتوانیِ ترسناک باشد، این می‌تواند نمونهٔ **جبران افراطی / جبران‌گر افراطی** باشد، به‌جای توالی تسلیم و اجتناب در مثال بالا. اگر فقط از قاعدهٔ «استراحت هرگز مجاز نیست» پیروی کند، باید قاعدهٔ پرتوقع و احتمالِ معیارهای سرسختانه را بررسی کرد. از روی تعداد ساعت‌های کار به‌تنهایی نمی‌توان هیچ‌یک را نتیجه گرفت. پاسخ متعادل می‌تواند انتخاب یک کار محدود، حفظ زمان استراحت و بازبینی برنامه هنگام تغییر توان باشد.

## 10 Examining your experience

Keep the shared eight fields; add prompts within them rather than a scored mode inventory. Ask about the first trigger and meaning, feelings/body clues, a possible mode, what changed, the response's function, immediate relief, later cost, and the need still present. “I am unsure” is a useful answer. Use Neda's sequence as a completed fictional worksheet.

Original prompts:

- **EN:** “What changed just before I switched from wanting relief to attacking myself?” **FA:** «درست پیش از آنکه از خواستن آرامش به سرزنش خودم برسم، چه چیزی تغییر کرد؟»
- **EN:** “Was I acting as if the belief were true, avoiding its feelings, or trying to prove it wrong?” **FA:** «آیا طوری عمل کردم که انگار آن باور درست است، از احساس‌هایش دور شدم یا سعی کردم خلافش را ثابت کنم؟»
- **EN:** “What else could explain this moment, and what helped on a different occasion?” **FA:** «چه توضیح دیگری ممکن است برای این لحظه وجود داشته باشد و در موقعیتی دیگر چه چیزی کمک کرد؟»

## 11 Practicing change

Use the shared six-field experiment planner. In Neda's fictional experiment, the cue is opening a nonurgent update while adequately rested. Prediction: “Unless I feel interested, I cannot return at all.” Small action: notice the urge, name the feeling, sort one note, then reassess. Support and limits: clear instructions, a planned stop, permission to rest or renegotiate if capacity is insufficient. Observed outcome: some work completed, boredom remained, and the unfinished part was communicated. Learning: returning was possible on this occasion; a shorter first step may help again, but this is not proof that every task is manageable.

Add an optional practice for the punitive turn: a factual repair sentence instead of global condemnation. Do not require prolonged distress tolerance, withdrawal of needed supports, or experiments involving consequential financial, medical, or safety decisions. Identify these as original exercises, not validated treatment protocols.

## 13 Reviewing progress

Look for noticing an urge earlier, distinguishing a needed break from disconnection, returning after interruption, communicating revised commitments, and shortening self-attack. Ask whether an old sequence became more flexible, even when the task was not finished. Healthy Adult responding can include accommodations, rest, and asking for help. Avoid a “perfect discipline” streak, obligatory mode tracking, or treating an uncertain label as failure.

**EN:** “Where did I gain a little more choice, and what support would make the next attempt more workable?”

**FA:** «کجا کمی امکان انتخاب بیشتری پیدا کردم و چه حمایتی تلاش بعدی را عملی‌تر می‌کند؟»
