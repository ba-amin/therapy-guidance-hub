# Defectiveness / Shame guide draft

Authoring outline for **نقص / شرم**. Follow the [shared standard](../schema-guide-standard.md) and [reusable template](../schema-guide-template.md). This draft guides future revision, not assessment.

## 1 Getting started

Offer the shared recognition, examination, and practice paths. Familiar experiences do not establish a diagnosis. Original opening:

**EN:** “A difficult moment can feel like a verdict on your whole self. This guide invites you to examine that interpretation with curiosity.”

**FA:** «گاهی یک لحظهٔ دشوار شبیه حکمی دربارهٔ تمام وجودمان احساس می‌شود. این راهنما دعوتی است برای بررسی این برداشت با کنجکاوی.»

## 2 Understanding this schema

Explain the pattern as feeling fundamentally flawed and fearing rejection if others know you closely. [1](#ref-1)

Original core-rule example: **“If they know the real me, they will reject me.”** / **«اگر خودِ واقعی مرا بشناسند، مرا طرد می‌کنند.»** Present this as a possible internal story, never a fact about the reader.

## 3 Healthy needs and flexible alternatives

Build a comparison table around dignity, belonging, privacy, and accountability. Contrast “I handled that badly” with “I am bad.” Include room for genuine mistakes, repair, and disagreement. Respectful feedback concerns behavior; humiliation and abuse must not become exercises in accepting criticism.

## 4 Recognizing the pattern

Group optional observations into thoughts, feelings, bodily responses, actions, and triggers. Invite noticing the urge to hide after a compliment or replay a conversation. These experiences have many possible explanations; avoid a symptom checklist.

## 5 Possible origins and unmet needs

Invite reflection on remembered messages about belonging, mistakes, difference, and acceptance. Include family, peers, school, and wider social experiences. Ask what support would have helped. Present possible histories as hypotheses; do not infer mistreatment, demand memories, or make caregivers the sole explanation.

## 6 How the cycle continues

Use the shared six-step expandable cycle. Label this sequence fictional:

1. A colleague requests clarification.
2. “They have seen what is wrong with me” brings shame and tension.
3. The person withdraws from discussion.
4. The sense of exposure briefly eases.
5. They miss a chance to clarify the request and hear balanced feedback.
6. The assumption that being known leads to rejection remains untested.

Add an optional interruption point: clarify the actual request before concluding what it means about identity. Connect the meaning and response steps to section 7's mode sequence. Ask whether shame is followed by self-attack, appeasing, withdrawal, or a drive to prove superiority; invite other explanations and “unclear.”

## 7 Coping styles and modes

### Separate the three ideas

A **schema / طرحواره** is the recurring meaning: “Being known will expose something unacceptable about me.” A **coping style / سبک مقابله‌ای** is a broad way of responding to that meaning; a **coping response / پاسخ مقابله‌ای** is the specific action or strategy used in one situation. A **mode / ذهنیت** describes the emotional and coping state active now. The same schema can involve different modes and coping responses. [3](#ref-3)

### Three possible coping functions

These are **original fictional illustrations / نمونه‌های فرضی تألیفی**, informed by the coping framework. Classify by the function of the response and the person's meaning, rather than behavior alone. [3](#ref-3)

| Coping style | Trigger and feared meaning | Possible response | Immediate function and possible later cost | A flexible alternative |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Surrender / تسلیم** | A friend makes a broad put-down after a minor mistake; “They see what I really am.” | Leila accepts the insult as a valid description of her whole self and repeatedly puts herself down in later conversations. | Ends uncertainty about the judgment by treating it as true; the global conclusion gains strength and respectful treatment can seem undeserved. | Identify the specific mistake and, if safe, say: “I can discuss what happened, but I will not continue while being insulted.” Seek support if direct objection is unsafe. |
| **Avoidance / اجتناب** | A considerate friend asks how she is doing; “If I answer honestly, they will discover the real me.” | Leila changes the subject or withdraws from contact whenever the conversation becomes personal. | Limits the immediate feeling of exposure; she loses opportunities for chosen closeness and remains unsure whether acceptance is possible. | Choose a small, bounded answer: “I have had a difficult day; I would like company but do not want to discuss the details.” |
| **Overcompensation / جبران افراطی** | A colleague questions a detail; “I must prove I am above criticism or they will see that I am inferior.” | Leila dismisses the colleague's ability and displays her achievements to establish superiority. | Briefly counters the feeling of inferiority; discussion becomes strained, and that distance can be read as further evidence that others will reject her. | Ask which detail needs clarification and answer that point, acknowledging uncertainty or correcting it where needed without comparing anyone's worth. |

Apologizing for a specific mistake, preserving privacy, and taking pride in work can each be healthy. Staying in a harmful situation can reflect constraints or danger; it does not establish surrender, and responsibility for mistreatment remains with the person causing it. Perfectionistic overwork can compensate for felt defectiveness while also surrendering to an Unrelenting Standards rule; specify which meaning is being examined. [3](#ref-3)

### Possible modes and how they might become noticeable

Names follow Young and First's mode listing. [2](#ref-2) The cues, inner sentences, bodily sensations, and alternatives below are **original fictional possibilities**, not assessment criteria. Mode labels describe temporary states, not a person's identity. The same modes can appear with other schemas; a coping style is not a complete mode description. “Child” and “Parent” terms do not prove a childhood event or identify a caregiver. A blush, withdrawal, tears, or anger alone cannot establish a Child mode.

| Possible mode | Example cue, inner speech, feeling/body, and action | Possible Healthy Adult response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Vulnerable Child / کودک آسیب‌پذیر** | A colleague asks for clarification. “Now they can see I do not belong.” Leila feels exposed and small, her face grows hot, and she wants reassurance or to hide. | “Feeling exposed is painful. One question does not settle my worth; I can check what is being asked.” |
| **Punitive Parent / والد تنبیه‌گر** | Remembering a mistake, “You are disgusting; you deserve to be excluded.” Shame and a sinking feeling accompany repeated self-insults. | “Accountability can be specific. Humiliating myself does not help me understand or repair what happened.” |
| **Demanding Parent / والد پرتوقع** | Preparing to join a discussion, “You must never say anything imperfect.” Pressure and a tight chest accompany rewriting an ordinary contribution over and over. | “I can prepare adequately and allow room for questions. Belonging does not have to be earned through flawless performance.” |
| **Compliant Surrenderer / تسلیم‌شوندهٔ مطیع** | Someone calls her useless. “They must be right; I should not object.” She feels resigned, lowers her gaze, and agrees with a global put-down. | “I can consider any specific feedback while declining humiliation, and seek support if responding directly is unsafe.” |
| **Detached Protector / محافظ جداشده** | A friend asks about her disappointment. “Switch off; do not let anyone near this.” Leila feels blank or disconnected and retreats into repeated distraction. | “I can choose privacy and still acknowledge my feelings. A small, safe connection is an option, not an obligation.” |
| **Overcompensator / جبران‌گر افراطی** | A question feels exposing. “I will show that I am better than them.” With a tense posture and defensive energy, she criticizes others or boasts to repel felt inferiority. | “I can respond to the actual question without having to place either of us above the other.” |
| **Healthy Adult / بزرگسال سالم** | After a mistake, “Something needs correcting; that does not make my whole self unacceptable.” Embarrassment remains, but she checks the facts, repairs what she can, and sets limits on insults. | Support this state through realistic information, kindness, and proportionate responsibility; it need not erase shame immediately. |

### A repeated sequence and an interruption

**Fictional example — EN:** A colleague asks Leila to clarify one sentence. She feels hot and exposed: “They have discovered I am fundamentally inadequate” (**possible Vulnerable Child**). “You should be ashamed of yourself” follows (**possible Punitive Parent**). She then stops joining informal discussions and repeatedly distracts herself to avoid feeling exposed (**avoidance; possible Detached Protector**). The immediate intensity drops, but she misses ordinary exchanges that might clarify the relationship. She interprets the resulting distance as confirmation that she does not belong. When a similar question arrives the next week, shame and withdrawal return. These labels are possibilities within this invented example, not conclusions from the colleague's request.

**An interruption — EN:** Leila notices the heat in her face and the move from embarrassment to self-attack. Her **Healthy Adult** response is, “This is a request about a sentence. I can ask which part is unclear and respond to that.” She may still feel embarrassed, and her wording may need correction. The experiment is to allow a specific conversation without turning it into a verdict on her whole self.

**مثال فرضی — FA:** همکار لیلا می‌خواهد یک جمله را روشن‌تر توضیح دهد. صورت لیلا داغ می‌شود و احساس می‌کند عیبش آشکار شده: «فهمیده‌اند که من از اساس مشکل دارم» (**احتمالاً ذهنیت کودک آسیب‌پذیر**). سپس می‌گوید: «باید از خودت خجالت بکشی» (**احتمالاً والد تنبیه‌گر**). از گفت‌وگوهای غیررسمی کنار می‌کشد و برای دورشدن از احساس آشکارشدن عیبش، مدام خود را سرگرم می‌کند (**اجتناب؛ احتمالاً محافظ جداشده**). شدت احساس برای مدتی کمتر می‌شود، اما فرصت تعامل‌های عادی که می‌توانستند رابطه را روشن‌تر کنند از دست می‌رود. فاصلهٔ ایجادشده را تأییدی بر این می‌گیرد که به جمع تعلق ندارد. هفتهٔ بعد، با پرسشی مشابه، شرم و کناره‌گیری برمی‌گردند. این نام‌ها فقط احتمال‌هایی در یک مثال ساختگی‌اند و از درخواست همکار نمی‌توان چنین نتیجه‌ای گرفت.

**نقطهٔ تغییر — FA:** لیلا داغی صورتش و حرکت از خجالت به سرزنش خود را متوجه می‌شود. پاسخ **بزرگسال سالم** او این است: «درخواست دربارهٔ یک جمله است. می‌توانم بپرسم کدام بخش روشن نیست و به همان پاسخ بدهم.» شاید هنوز خجالت بکشد و جمله‌اش هم به اصلاح نیاز داشته باشد. هدف تمرین این است که دربارهٔ موضوعی مشخص گفت‌وگو کند، بدون اینکه آن را به حکمی دربارهٔ تمام وجودش تبدیل کند.

**Alternative branch — EN:** With the same request for clarification, Leila might ridicule the colleague's reading ability to push away her own sense of inferiority (**overcompensation; possible Overcompensator**). A firm explanation or disagreement can be healthy; the question is whether the response addresses the sentence or tries to defeat felt defectiveness by putting someone else down.

**مسیر احتمالی دیگر — FA:** در برابر همان درخواست توضیح، لیلا ممکن است توانایی همکار در خواندن متن را مسخره کند تا احساس حقارت خودش را دور نگه دارد (**جبران افراطی؛ احتمالاً جبران‌گر افراطی**). توضیح قاطع یا مخالفت می‌تواند سالم باشد؛ پرسش این است که واکنش به همان جمله می‌پردازد یا می‌کوشد با تحقیر دیگری بر احساس نقص غلبه کند.

## 8 Everyday examples

Provide expandable fictional examples across work, friendship, dating, study, parenting, and appearance. Use the shared trigger–meaning–response–alternative structure.

**Worked example:** Leila forgets a friend's birthday. “I am a terrible person” leads her to avoid replying. A flexible response is, “I missed something important; I can apologize and ask how to repair it.” Her friend may still be disappointed. The alternative supports responsibility without requiring reassurance or a flawless outcome.

For each example, add the coping function, tentative mode clues, and any shift before or after the response. For the birthday example, ask whether self-attack preceded avoidance; do not assume it did. Include a contrasting healthy apology and chosen privacy so readers can distinguish function from appearance. Link one worked example to section 7's repeated sequence.

## 9 Related patterns

Distinguish failure, social isolation, approval seeking, and unrelenting standards by feared meaning. Separate global self-condemnation from guilt about specific actions. Explain overlap without diagnosing a personality, anxiety, or mood disorder from a schema description.

## 10 Examining your experience

Use the shared unscored worksheet. Original prompts: “What happened?” “What conclusion did I draw about myself?” “What was actually said?” “What did hiding or proving myself accomplish?” “What support or boundary would help?” Preserve one input set across languages and explain that answers remain only in the page.

Add optional mode cues inside the existing fields: field 3, “What state and body cue appeared first?”; field 4, “Did I accept a global judgment, escape exposure, or try to establish superiority? Did my response change?”; field 5, “What eased briefly and what later seemed to confirm the belief?”; field 7, “How could I respond to the specific issue while respecting my worth?” Allow an ordinary-language description or “unclear” instead of a mode name.

**FA prompt:** «اول چه احساس و حالت بدنی‌ای داشتم؟ آیا بعد به سرزنش خود، کناره‌گیری یا تلاش برای اثبات برتری تغییر کرد؟ واکنشم چه فایدهٔ فوری و چه پیامد بعدی داشت؟»

## 11 Practicing change

Draft an optional experiment: ask one ordinary clarification question in a respectful setting. Record the feared response, outcome, and uncertainty. This tests a prediction, not personal worth. Do not require intimate disclosure or interaction with someone who mistreats the reader.

Connect the plan to one early cue from section 7 and one possible interruption: when embarrassment starts turning into self-attack, name the actual task and ask the clarification question. Record any urge to withdraw or prove superiority afterward, along with a supportive response. Success is a considered, bounded action and useful observation; it does not require naming every mode or feeling confident.

## 12 How therapy can help

Describe assessment and a collaborative understanding of the pattern before proposing change; reference the practitioner text's introductory overview. [3](#ref-3) Reserve imagery and chair-work explanations for therapist-supported practice. Avoid scripts inviting readers to relive distressing experiences alone.

## 13 Reviewing progress

Suggest noticing recovery after embarrassment, proportionate repair, clearer boundaries, and greater choice about sharing. Include the existing formulation summary here. Explicitly avoid making confidence, openness, or absence of shame new perfectionistic demands. Allow “I noticed it afterward” to count as useful learning.

Review the sequence rather than counting “bad” modes: “Was the turn from shame to self-attack easier to notice? Could I interrupt withdrawal, keep a chosen boundary, or respond to the specific issue sooner?” Look for more flexible transitions and less self-condemnation, with no requirement to remove vulnerable feelings.

## 14 What research supports

Separate model descriptions from observational associations and treatment outcomes. Before adding efficacy claims, verify the primary study, participants, comparator, outcomes, and limitations. Broad schema-therapy findings must not imply this specific guide has been tested. Mark original exercises and fictional examples accordingly.
