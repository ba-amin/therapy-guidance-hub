# Schema guide authoring standard draft

Use the same reading journey for every guide: **understand the pattern, notice it in context, examine one experience, try a manageable response, and review what happened**. Keep the clinical focus specific to each schema while making navigation, examples, worksheets, citations, and language controls familiar.

This standard provides a structure for the four guides in the hub and future guides. All four schema outlines below are drafting resources; changes to these documents do not automatically update the HTML guides. The two older pages have not yet been fully migrated to this structure. The Insufficient Self-Control / Self-Discipline guide follows the fifteen-section template.

## Audience and writing approach

Write for adults exploring their own experience, including readers unfamiliar with therapy. Use ordinary language before introducing a technical term. Address patterns as possibilities: “You might notice…” and “Consider whether…” leave room for disagreement and different circumstances.

Give each explanation a practical purpose. A reader should be able to distinguish an event, its interpretation, an emotion, a coping response, and a need. Present modes as temporary patterns of experience and response, without implying that each reader has a fixed set of parts. Schema Therapy distinguishes schemas, coping responses, and modes; these terms should not be used interchangeably. [1](#ref-1)

Use a short explanation followed by a concrete illustration when the concept needs one. Put optional depth in expandable content. Keep essential definitions, exercise instructions, and relevant limitations visible. Avoid turning every paragraph into a card or asking a reflection question after every fact.

Offer three reading paths near the beginning:

- **Recognize:** sections 1–4, followed by an everyday example in section 8.
- **Examine:** the cycle in section 6, coping styles and modes in section 7, and the worksheet in section 10.
- **Practice:** sections 11–13, with research and sources available in sections 14–15.

References are the final content section. Place closing reflections, summary maps, and next steps earlier. A footer may contain navigation, but should not introduce another clinical explanation after the references.

For section 3, compare chosen cooperation with fearful submission in Subjugation, behavior-specific accountability with global self-condemnation in Defectiveness/Shame, and flexible ambition with rigid demands in Unrelenting Standards. These are different teaching comparisons within the same section role.

Do not assume a particular childhood, rank relatives' responsibility, or infer a cause from a checklist. Identify the source's needs framework before using it; the hub's six-item overview and other authors' groupings should not be silently treated as identical.

Describe what a behavior is trying to accomplish before assigning a coping label. In particular, distinguish obeying a demanding standard from using achievement to counter a different fear. Do not equate healthy boundary setting with overcompensation. Review each proposed formulation against the conceptual source and the individual example. [1](#ref-1)

## Required coverage of coping styles and modes

Section 7 must be explicit in every individual schema guide. A list of mode names or a generic paragraph about coping is insufficient. Help the reader answer: **What activates this pattern, how does it feel, what do I do with it, and how might the sequence repeat?** Use the following structure in the [reusable template](schema-guide-template.md#7-coping-styles-and-modes).

### Distinguish the concepts

A schema is the enduring theme; a coping style describes a broad way of responding to it; a coping response is a particular action or mental strategy; a mode is the state active at a given moment. Modes can involve schemas, emotions, and coping responses. One person may respond differently across situations and shift modes within a situation. [1](#ref-1) Explain these distinctions briefly with the guide's own example.

Do not present a schema-to-mode lookup as a diagnosis. A coping mode can illustrate a style, but child modes and parent modes are not themselves three additional coping styles. Avoid inferring a particular mode from an isolated action. [1](#ref-1) Explain that “Child” and “Parent” are model terms; readers can use ordinary descriptions such as “feeling exposed” or “pressuring myself.” Do not infer a childhood event or a literal parent's intentions from a mode label.

### Show all three coping styles by function

Include one original, explicitly fictional example of **surrender**, **avoidance**, and **overcompensation** for this schema. Each example must identify the trigger and meaning, the response, its intended immediate function, a possible later cost, and a flexible alternative. Keep “what happened” separate from “what we think it was doing.” If the function is uncertain, say so.

Use these concise distinctions: surrender follows the schema as if it were true; avoidance reduces contact with its activation or associated experience; overcompensation counters it in an excessive, rigid way. [1](#ref-1) Ask how the particular action serves that function. Do not label all hard work as overcompensation, all rest as avoidance, or all cooperation as surrender. Account for actual safety, choice, capacity, and context.

### Make mode activation recognizable

Select relevant modes and include Healthy Adult. Consider vulnerable or frustrated states, coping modes, and demanding or punitive internal messages where they fit; do not force every family into every example. For each selected mode, provide:

- A brief cited explanation and its English/Persian name.
- A schema-specific situation that could activate it and an original internal sentence.
- Possible feelings, bodily cues, and actions, identified as illustrations rather than criteria.
- The need or difficulty to address, and a concrete Healthy Adult response.

The Young–First listing separates Demanding Parent pressure from Punitive Parent punishment and describes Healthy Adult care alongside limits and responsibility. [2](#ref-2) Show these differences in dialogue rather than treating every unpleasant thought as the same mode. Healthy Adult is a way of responding, not an expectation to be calm or productive at all times. Give a brief counterexample: similar outward behavior could have a different meaning, including a healthy one.

### Show what a recurring sequence could look like

Provide one complete fictional sequence that connects **trigger → meaning and activated state → coping response → immediate effect → later cost or mode shift → reinforced expectation at the next trigger**. Name the proposed coping function and mode changes where the example supports them; allow mixed states and uncertainty. Add a brief alternative branch showing a different coping style after the same starting trigger. Neither branch is a universal order or a prediction about the reader.

Make repetition concrete: show what the person concludes afterward, how that affects a later situation, and what keeps the loop going. Immediate relief is one possibility; surrender can retain distress while making the familiar expectation seem confirmed. Put one Healthy Adult interruption at an identifiable point, with an original sentence, an action, and a need it serves. A mixed outcome is more useful than a guaranteed success.

Reuse the section 6 situation and the section 10 completed worksheet where practical, so readers can follow one experience across the guide. Keep the full sequence in section 6 or 7 and link to it from the other; do not repeat the full story. In section 8, show a second setting or a different response; in section 11, plan for the state that may make the experiment difficult; in section 13, review noticing, switching, repair, and recovery without scoring modes.

### Check coverage before publication

An individual guide is ready on this point only when:

- All three styles have distinct examples whose functions fit the schema.
- Mode examples include activation cues, internal speech, feelings/body, actions, and a need or response; Healthy Adult is explicit.
- A complete recurring sequence and a different branch show how styles and modes relate without equating them.
- Recognition and worksheet prompts invite a tentative label or “not sure / does not fit”; no mode score or compulsory classification appears.
- English and Persian preserve the same functions, sequences, qualifications, and citations.
- Definitions cite consulted conceptual sources; original examples are not presented as observed prevalence, diagnostic criteria, or tested treatments.

## Reusable examples and exercises

Label invented material **“Fictional example” / “مثال فرضی”** and original exercises **“Original reflection exercise” / “تمرین تأملی تألیفی”**. The labels identify their status without presenting them as validated assessment tools.

Use the same worked-example sequence:

1. Describe an observable situation without interpreting anyone's motives.
2. Show the person's automatic meaning or internal rule.
3. Name feelings and bodily cues, a possible activated mode, and the action they take; explain the coping function before suggesting a style.
4. Explain the immediate function and a possible later cost.
5. Identify a need and a more flexible response.
6. Show any subsequent mode shift, what could repeat at the next trigger, and what could be learned from an uncertain or mixed outcome.

Use varied contexts such as work, study, friendship, family, household responsibilities, and rest. Avoid making one gender, culture, occupation, or family arrangement the default explanation. Show that the same outward behavior may have different meanings.

The maintenance cycle uses six expandable steps: **trigger → meaning or rule → coping response → immediate effect → later cost → reinforcement**. Add tentative mode names and feelings/body cues within the relevant steps, including a shift after a consequence; keep six steps rather than adding a separate mode diagram with a different story. Add a clearly labeled alternative at one point in the cycle; it should illustrate a possible choice rather than promise a particular outcome. Essential labels and the sequence remain visible; expandable content provides detail.

Use one reflection worksheet with these eight fields:

| Field | English prompt | Persian prompt |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | What happened, and what triggered the reaction? | چه اتفاقی افتاد و چه چیزی واکنش را برانگیخت؟ |
| 2 | What did the situation seem to mean? What rule appeared? | موقعیت برایم چه معنایی داشت؟ چه قاعده‌ای در ذهنم ظاهر شد؟ |
| 3 | What feelings and bodily sensations did I notice? | چه احساس‌ها و حس‌های بدنی را متوجه شدم؟ |
| 4 | What did I do, and what was that response trying to achieve? | چه کردم و آن واکنش قرار بود چه چیزی را فراهم یا از چه چیزی جلوگیری کند؟ |
| 5 | What happened immediately, and what were the later costs? | پیامد فوری چه بود و بعداً چه هزینه‌هایی داشت؟ |
| 6 | What did I need in that situation? | در آن موقعیت به چه چیزی نیاز داشتم؟ |
| 7 | What alternative could respect my needs and the situation? | چه پاسخ دیگری می‌توانست هم نیازهایم و هم شرایط را در نظر بگیرد؟ |
| 8 | What happened next, or what support would help? | بعد چه شد، یا چه حمایتی می‌تواند کمک کند؟ |

Use six experiment-planning fields: **situation and limits; prediction; small action; support or stopping conditions; observed outcome; learning and next adjustment**. Tailor the example to the schema. A disappointing outcome can be useful information; it does not prove a negative belief about the person.

Add these optional helper prompts inside the existing worksheet fields, preserving the eight reflection inputs and six experiment inputs. Readers may use everyday descriptions, write “not sure,” or skip them. Do not require a mode dropdown or add an automatic classifier.

| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 — Meaning | What was the first internal sentence or rule? | اولین جمله یا قاعدهٔ درونی چه بود؟ |
| 3 — Feelings and body | What state seemed active? Did it change? A mode name is optional. | چه حالتی فعال به نظر می‌رسید؟ آیا تغییر کرد؟ نام‌گذاری ذهنیت اختیاری است. |
| 4 — Response and function | Was I following the belief, avoiding the experience, or trying rigidly to prove its opposite? It may be unclear. | آیا مطابق باور عمل می‌کردم، از تجربه دور می‌شدم، یا می‌کوشیدم به‌شکلی خشک خلاف آن را ثابت کنم؟ ممکن است روشن نباشد. |
| 5 — Consequences | What state followed, and what expectation might carry into the next situation? | پس از آن چه حالتی پدید آمد و چه انتظاری ممکن است به موقعیت بعدی منتقل شود؟ |
| 7 — Alternative | What could a caring, realistic Healthy Adult response say and do here? | پاسخ مراقبت‌گر و واقع‌بینانهٔ بزرگسال سالم در این موقعیت چه می‌توانست بگوید و انجام دهد؟ |
| 8 — Outcome or support | Which early cue or support could help me notice the sequence next time? | کدام نشانهٔ اولیه یا حمایت می‌تواند کمک کند دفعهٔ بعد متوجه این توالی شوم؟ |

In the experiment planner, use the prediction field for the expected mode or coping urge, the support field for a planned response, and the learning field for what shifted or repeated. An unfinished experiment can still reveal a useful cue.

Recognition prompts remain optional and unscored. Do not convert checked items into percentages, severity labels, or diagnoses. Avoid assignments involving major consequences, unsafe relationships, or the relaxation of real safety obligations.

## Evidence and citations

Keep a small authoring record for each substantive claim: proposed wording, exact source passage or page, evidence type, population, and relevant limitation. This is a drafting aid, not extra content the reader must navigate.

Distinguish conceptual explanations, observational associations, treatment trials, and supplementary practical resources. A trial of a complete treatment does not automatically validate an isolated exercise or this interactive guide. When summarizing a study, include its comparison and outcome rather than simply saying that treatment “works.”

Place numbered links beside supported claims and link them to `ref-1`, `ref-2`, and so on. Give the same claim the same reference number in both languages. The final bibliography should include authors, year, title, publisher or journal details, DOI or stable URL, and relevant edition information. Cite the chapter, abstract, or article actually consulted; put unread books in further reading. Original paraphrases and prompts must not reproduce copyrighted questionnaires.

## Language and interaction conventions

Use English and Persian with equivalent meaning, including uncertainty, qualifications, citations, controls, and feedback. Agree on a glossary for schema names, coping styles, and modes before translating. Introduce a technical term with an ordinary-language explanation and use the chosen term consistently thereafter.

Persian remains the default. Set `lang` and `dir` correctly on the document, preserve the shared language preference, and update button states accessibly. Use `.content-fa` and `.content-en` for paired text, with **one set of input elements** so language switching preserves answers. Isolate English titles and DOIs within Persian text where needed.

Store only the language preference under `therapy-guidance-lang`. Worksheet responses remain in the current page; do not submit them, persist them, place them in URLs, or include them in analytics. Clear each worksheet independently and announce the result. Describe this behavior briefly beside the forms, including that reloading can discard answers.

Prefer native `<details>` and `<summary>` for new expandable explanations. Use real buttons for actions, explicit input labels, visible keyboard focus, and a skip link. All essential content must remain readable without expanding an explanation.

Printing must include the selected language, expanded explanations, references, and the complete text of worksheet responses. Use print text mirrors rather than relying on the visible height of a textarea. Restore disclosure states after printing.
