## 1. Young, Klosko, and Weishaar — conceptual framework

<a id="ref-1"></a>
Young, J. E., Klosko, J. S., & Weishaar, M. E. (2003). *Schema Therapy: A Practitioner's Guide*. Guilford Press. [Official Chapter 1 excerpt](https://www.guilford.com/excerpts/young.pdf?t=1).

- **Access:** official excerpt only, not the complete book. Page locators below are printed pages; zero-based PDF indices are one less.
- **Locators:** pp. 9–10, core emotional needs and their conceptual status; pp. 17 and 21, definition; p. 20, Overvigilance and Inhibition; pp. 22–23, conditional schemas; pp. 32–36, coping; Table 1.1, p. 39, Unrelenting Standards row; pp. 37–44, modes; pp. 44–47, treatment approaches.
- **Supported claim:** damaging internal demands can displace enjoyment and connection. Spontaneity/play and expression of valid needs have direct conceptual relevance; other needs depend on the individual's formulation.
- **Coping precision:** the table places excessive perfectionistic effort under surrender, avoiding evaluated tasks under avoidance, and rigid rejection of standards under overcompensation. Achievement used against another schema can have another function.
- **Limit:** theoretical/clinical framework, not proof of a fixed need-to-schema mapping, an individual's upbringing, or this guide's efficacy. Do not import historical efficacy or memory claims as current findings. The Sara sequence is original.

## 3. International Society of Schema Therapy — definitions and comparisons

<a id="ref-3"></a>
International Society of Schema Therapy. (n.d.). *Schema Therapy Central Concepts*. [Official overview](https://schematherapysociety.org/Schema-Therapy).

- **Access/locators:** full webpage; domain Overvigilance and Inhibition and schema 17; comparison definitions 5 (Defectiveness/Shame), 9 (Failure), 14 (Approval-Seeking/Recognition-Seeking), 16 (Emotional Inhibition), and 18 (Punitiveness).
- **Supported claim:** schema 17 centers on rigid internalized demands with significant costs, rather than achievement alone. Its listed expressions include perfectionism, rigid rules, and efficiency concerns. Related schemas differ in central meaning even when actions look similar.
- **Use:** opening brief, healthy contrast, and same-situation comparison. Original examples illustrate possibilities, not empirical associations between schemas.
- **Limit:** professional conceptual resource, not a diagnostic checklist, treatment trial, or proof of a reader's history. Cultural values and real obligations need contextual consideration.

## 4. Young and First — mode terminology

<a id="ref-4"></a>
Young, J. E., & First, M. (2003). *Schema Mode Listing*. Schema Therapy Institute. [Official listing](https://www.schematherapy.com/id72.htm).

- **Access/locators:** listing entries 1 and 5–10, inspected through available page/indexed text; the web text extractor truncates some versions. [Same-site indexed copy](https://mail.schematherapy.com/id72.htm).
- **Supported claim:** distinguish Vulnerable Child, Compliant Surrenderer, Detached Protector, Overcompensator, Punitive Parent, Demanding or Critical Parent, and Healthy Adult. Demanding concerns internalized rules; punitive concerns how they are enforced.
- **Use:** the hub's established English/Persian glossary; “Demanding Parent” is its shortened label. Cues, bodily sensations, and dialogue in the guide are original, nonspecific illustrations.
- **Limit:** mode terminology is not an assessment or a compulsory set of states. A parent-mode name does not identify a literal parent's motives; obeying one's own standard does not automatically establish interpersonal Compliant Surrenderer mode.

## 6. Bamelis and colleagues — whole-treatment trial

<a id="ref-6"></a>
Bamelis, L. L. M., Evers, S. M. A. A., Spinhoven, P., & Arntz, A. (2014). Results of a multicenter randomized controlled trial of the clinical effectiveness of schema therapy for personality disorders. *American Journal of Psychiatry, 171*(3), 305–322. [DOI: 10.1176/appi.ajp.2013.12040518](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2013.12040518). [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24322378/).

- **Access/locators:** abstract and bibliographic metadata, especially Objective, Method, and Results; full article not reviewed for this revision.
- **Design/population:** single-blind multicenter randomized trial at 12 Dutch institutes; 323 patients with cluster C, paranoid, histrionic, or narcissistic personality disorders. Schema Therapy n = 147; usual treatment n = 135; clarification-oriented psychotherapy n = 41.
- **Finding:** the 50-session Schema Therapy condition had better diagnostic recovery three years after treatment started than comparison conditions. Interview-based findings favored Schema Therapy; self-report measures did not show between-treatment differences.
- **Limit/use:** whole-treatment evidence in a clinical population. It does not establish an Unrelenting Standards-specific treatment, the effect of one technique, or the effectiveness of this guide and its worksheets. Avoid adding effect estimates unavailable in the inspected abstract.

## 7. Koerner and colleagues — schema-specific association

<a id="ref-7"></a>
Koerner, N., Tallon, K., & Kusec, A. (2015). Maladaptive core beliefs and their relation to generalized anxiety disorder. *Cognitive Behaviour Therapy, 44*(6), 441–455. [DOI: 10.1080/16506073.2015.1042989](https://doi.org/10.1080/16506073.2015.1042989). [PubMed abstract and metadata](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26029983/).

- **Access/locator:** abstract only, methods/results paragraphs; full article not reviewed.
- **Design/population:** observational self-report study, N = 138, classified into probable GAD and non-GAD groups. No treatment was assigned.
- **Careful wording:** Unrelenting Standards was among the unique correlates of probable GAD status **or** symptom severity after adjustment for trait anxiety and depressive symptoms.
- **Limit/use:** association is not causation or a demonstrated mechanism. The abstract groups outcomes with “or”; do not claim an independent association with both outcomes. Probable GAD is not a confirmed clinical diagnosis. Do not reproduce the questionnaire examples quoted in the abstract or infer a threshold for the guide's original prompts.

## 8. Jeong and colleagues — perfectionism in a digital intervention

<a id="ref-8"></a>
Jeong, S., Kim, H., Lho, S. K., Hwang, I., Mun, S., Kim, S., Lim, H., Kim, H., Shin, M.-S., & Moon, W. (2025). Schema-informed digital mental health intervention for maladaptive cognitive-emotional patterns: Randomized controlled trial. *Journal of Medical Internet Research, 27*, e65892. [DOI: 10.2196/65892](https://doi.org/10.2196/65892). [Corrected full article](https://www.jmir.org/2025/1/e65892).

- **Access/locators:** corrected full article; Methods—Program-Specific Modules, Assessment, Primary Outcomes; Results—Tables 1–3, Riggy Program, Maintenance of Intervention Effects; Discussion—Limitations. Use the corrected article rather than earlier versions.
- **Design/population:** 10-week multicomponent digital program versus waitlist. Across four modules, 300 adults were randomized and 218 completers analyzed. The perfectionism module, Riggy, included **54 analyzed participants: 34 intervention and 20 waitlist** (Table 2); this is not a 300-person perfectionism trial.
- **Finding:** analyzed Riggy participants reported lower perfectionism than waitlist at weeks 5 and 10 after age adjustment (Table 3). The measure was the Hewitt Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale, not the YSQ.
- **Limits:** completion-only analysis, baseline age imbalance, predominantly female/educated sample, inactive comparison, and one-month follow-up only in the intervention group. Schema change and the separate contribution of Schema Therapy or personalization were not established.
- **Reporting precision:** use counts rather than copying inconsistent completion percentages in the prose. Preserve the distinction between perfectionism outcomes and direct schema measurement.

<a id="ref-9"></a>
Fursland, A., Raykos, B., & Steele, A. (2009). *Perfectionism in Perspective*. Centre for Clinical Interventions, Perth, Western Australia. ISBN 0-975799576. [Official resources](https://cci.health.wa.gov.au/Resources/Looking-After-Yourself/Perfectionism).

- **Access/locators:** resource page and [Module 5, Reducing my Perfectionist Behaviour](https://cci.health.wa.gov.au/~/media/CCI/Consumer-Modules/Perfectionism-in-Perspective/Perfectionism-in-Perspective--05--Reducing-my-Perfectionist-Behaviour.pdf); [Module 7, Adjusting Unhelpful Rules & Assumptions](https://cci.health.wa.gov.au/~/media/CCI/Consumer-Modules/Perfectionism-in-Perspective/Perfectionism-in-Perspective---07---Adjusting-unhelpful-rules-and-assumptions.pdf), especially pp. 2–7 and credits p. 11. The latter identifies the authors and March 2009 date.
- **Supported claim:** flexible standards and proportionate behavior experiments can be considered without abandoning useful commitments. Procrastination may accompany fear of imperfect work.
- **Limit/use:** supplementary CBT self-help material, not Schema Therapy outcome evidence. The page's 2019 update is not the workbook publication date. The guide's eight/six-field worksheets are original and do not reproduce CCI's worksheets.
