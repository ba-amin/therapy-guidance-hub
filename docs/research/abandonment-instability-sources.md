Source/content review, independent cross-review of both guide halves, English/Persian equivalence, all fifteen sections, forty browser acceptance checks, local links, responsive layouts, and printing were checked. The detailed results below distinguish editorial review from technical checks. This is an editorial evidence record, not independent clinical validation.

All characters, scenarios, inner sentences, mode cues, recognition prompts, worksheets, and communication examples are original educational material. ## 1. Young, Klosko, and Weishaar — conceptual foundation

<a id="ref-1"></a>
Young, J. E., Klosko, J. S., & Weishaar, M. E. (2003). *Schema Therapy: A Practitioner's Guide*. Guilford Press. [Official Chapter 1 excerpt](https://www.guilford.com/excerpts/young.pdf?t=1).

- **Access:** relevant passages in the official Chapter 1 PDF, not the full book. Locators are printed page numbers; zero-based PDF indices are one lower.
- **Definition/domain locators:** printed p. 13, “Domain I: Disconnection and Rejection,” and Figure 1.1 on p. 14. The concern is anticipated instability of important connection, including unpredictable presence or feared departure/death. Comparison definitions occur on pp. 13–15 and 18.
- **Needs/origins locators:** pp. 9–12, “Core Emotional Needs,” “Early Life Experiences,” and “Emotional Temperament.” Five broad need groupings are listed on p. 10. Safety, stability, nurturance, and acceptance are components of secure attachment, not extra formal need groups. Page 9's historical footnote describes the list's then-current clinical/theoretical status; do not turn that into a claim that no later empirical research exists.
- **Coping/mode locators:** pp. 32–36, the three coping styles; Table 1.1 on p. 38, Abandonment/Instability row; pp. 37–44, state-like mode activation and mode descriptions. Table 1.1 must be read across the columns: persistently unavailable relationships illustrate surrender, avoiding intimacy illustrates avoidance, and excessive clinging or attacks around separation illustrate overcompensation.
- **Treatment locators:** pp. 44–47, “Schema Assessment and Change,” “Cognitive Techniques,” “Experiential Techniques,” “Behavioral Pattern-Breaking,” and “The Therapy Relationship.” These support collaborative formulation and a combination of cognitive, experiential, behavioral, and relational work. The experiential overview names imagery and dialogues; it does not provide a reviewed chair-work protocol here.
- **Use:** the guide's definition, core needs, cautious developmental discussion, function-based coping, six-mode teaching sequence, and brief therapy overview. Mina and Leila are original characters, not adaptations of a published clinical case.
- **Limits:** a clinical model does not prove any reader's cause, the accuracy of a particular memory, or this guide's efficacy. Do not repeat the excerpt's older broad efficacy assertions as current treatment findings. Do not infer that a person caused another person's mistreatment through temperament or coping.

## 2. International Society of Schema Therapy — definitions and domain map

<a id="ref-2"></a>
International Society of Schema Therapy. (n.d.). *Schema Therapy Central Concepts*. [Official webpage](https://schematherapysociety.org/Schema-Therapy).

- **Access/locators:** introductory four-concept explanation; Disconnection & Rejection domain; schema 1, Abandonment/Instability; comparison entries 2–4 and 6 for Mistrust/Abuse, Emotional Deprivation, Defectiveness/Shame, and Dependence/Incompetence.
- **Use:** independently verify the opening definition and domain; distinguish a fear about continuity from concerns about inadequate care, harm, personal unworthiness, or inability to manage tasks.
- **Precision:** preserve Young's five-group needs framework from reference 1. The webpage's brief introductory needs labels are not a replacement framework to silently combine with it. The domain groups related meanings; it is not a diagnosis or one of five established causal pathways.
- **Limits:** professional conceptual guidance, not an assessment test or a direct observational finding. The site's descriptions of possible origins do not establish an individual's childhood. The guide's counterexamples concerning bereavement, real unreliability, practical assistance, culture, and ordinary reassurance are contextual teaching applications.

## 3. Young and First — mode terminology

<a id="ref-3"></a>
Young, J. E., & First, M. (2003). *Schema Mode Listing*. Schema Therapy Institute. [Author-hosted listing](https://www.schematherapy.com/id72.htm).

- **Access/locators:** complete listing and final 2003 author attribution; especially entries 1, 2, 6, 7, 8, and 10.
- **Use:** Vulnerable Child / کودک آسیب‌پذیر; Angry Child / کودک خشمگین; Detached Protector / محافظ جداشده; Overcompensator / جبران‌گر افراطی; Punitive Parent / والد تنبیه‌گر; Healthy Adult / بزرگسال سالم. These match the repository glossary.
- **Precision:** anger around an unmet need and a controlling attempt to reverse vulnerability are distinct possible functions; an angry feeling alone does not establish either mode. Punitive Parent concerns blame or punishment, while Healthy Adult combines care with limits and responsibility. A mode is a temporary formulation, not a required part or a fixed personality identity.
- **Limits:** terminology, not a validated diagnostic checklist. The guide's inner sentences, bodily cues, and shifts are original applications, not items from this listing. Do not reproduce the source's descriptive inventories or attribute later subtypes to it.

## 4. Brockman and colleagues — contemporary conceptual chapter

<a id="ref-4"></a>
Brockman, R. N., Simpson, S., Hayes, C., van der Wijngaart, R., & Smout, M. (2023). From core emotional needs to schemas, coping styles, and schema modes: The conceptual model of schema therapy. In *Cambridge Guide to Schema Therapy* (pp. 1–15). Cambridge University Press. [DOI: 10.1017/9781108918145.003](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108918145.003). [Consulted full publisher chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-guide-to-schema-therapy/from-core-emotional-needs-to-schemas-coping-styles-and-schema-modes/721F16740657C4AD7C054AD8A7E3D9B1).

- **Access:** full chapter HTML, including both conceptual tables and treatment/model-development discussion; not the entire book or other clinical chapters. DOI resolution and a PDF route were unreliable during checking, but the direct publisher HTML provided the chapter text. Do not describe this access as PDF-only or summary-only.
- **Exact locators:** “The Heart of Schema Therapy: Core Emotional Needs”; “The Influence of Attachment Theory in Schema Therapy”; Table 1.1, Disconnection and Rejection; “Three Broad Maladaptive Coping Styles”; “The Schema Mode Model”; Table 1.2; “Schema Therapy Treatment Model”; “Recent Developments in the Model.” Named subsection locators are used because the consulted HTML does not expose page boundaries.
- **Use:** connect attachment needs, schema meanings, coping functions, and temporary modes. The chapter explicitly gives accepting insecure relationships as surrender. It also gives repeated reassurance/checking as surrender when the response follows the abandonment expectation.
- **Editorial reconciliation:** reference 1's table includes excessive clinging/attacking separations under overcompensation; this chapter includes reassurance/checking under surrender. The guide therefore does not attach a fixed style to all checking. Mina's overcompensation is explicitly a rigid attempt to force certainty by controlling another person's availability; a following-the-belief formulation may fit a different episode. Appearance alone cannot decide.
- **Model boundary:** the chapter describes proposed newer needs, schemas, mode terms, and groupings. This guide keeps the repository's familiar five-group/five-domain vocabulary and original mode glossary; it does not present later proposals as part of Young's 2003 list or as universally established categories.
- **Limits:** a clinical conceptual chapter, not a study of the guide or its exercises. The guide does not borrow the chapter's broad treatment-efficacy statements as new outcome evidence or claim to have reviewed the later intervention chapters.

## 5. D'Rozario and Pilkington — schema-specific observational evidence

<a id="ref-5"></a>
D'Rozario, A. B., & Pilkington, P. D. (2022). Parental separation or divorce and adulthood attachment: The mediating role of the Abandonment schema. *Clinical Psychology & Psychotherapy, 29*(2), 664–675. [DOI: 10.1002/cpp.2659](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpp.2659). [Consulted PubMed abstract and metadata](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34396641/). [Publisher bibliographic record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/cpp.2659).

- **Access:** abstract and bibliographic metadata only. The full methods, participant characteristics, statistical tables, and limitations section were not reviewed. PubMed's indexed abstract and the publisher record were used to verify the citation; direct extraction was intermittent.
- **Publication date:** the journal issue is **2022**; the version of record appeared online in September 2021. Use 2022 in the bibliography rather than treating the online and issue versions as separate studies.
- **Design/population reported in the abstract:** an observational online-measures study with **426 adults**, mean age 30.78 years. The summary reports associations and a mediation analysis, not a treatment allocation or follow-up intervention.
- **Finding/locator:** abstract Results. Reported parental separation/divorce was positively associated with anxious and avoidant attachment; the abandonment measure was involved in the statistical mediation of both relationships.
- **Limits:** associations and statistical mediation do not establish temporal or developmental causation. The abstract does not justify inferring universal effects of divorce, a specific person's history, representativeness, or unreported effect sizes. Its suggestions about limited reparenting are proposed clinical implications, not treatment results.
- **Use:** a narrow example of direct research on this schema, with the abstract-only access limitation visible in the guide. Do not describe this as evidence that healing this schema has been demonstrated to improve attachment security in this sample.

## 6. Arntz and colleagues — whole-treatment randomized trial

<a id="ref-6"></a>
Arntz, A., Jacob, G. A., Lee, C. W., Brand-de Wilde, O. M., Fassbinder, E., Harper, R. P., Lavender, A., Lockwood, G., Malogiannis, I. A., Ruths, F. A., Schweiger, U., Shaw, I. A., Zarbock, G., & Farrell, J. M. (2022). Effectiveness of predominantly group schema therapy and combined individual and group schema therapy for borderline personality disorder: A randomized clinical trial. *JAMA Psychiatry, 79*(4), 287–299. [DOI: 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2022.0010](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2022.0010). [Consulted full publisher article](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2789694).

- **Access/locators:** full article, especially Methods “Patients,” “Randomization and Masking,” “Treatments and Therapists”; Results “Primary Outcomes,” Tables 2–3; Discussion “Limitations” and “Conclusions.” Supplements are not represented as separately reviewed.
- **Design/population:** randomized outpatient trial across 15 sites in five countries. **495 adults with borderline personality disorder were randomized; 494 were analyzed** after one person withdrew consent. Participants were predominantly women. Treatment lasted two years, with assessment through three years.
- **Comparisons:** combined individual and group Schema Therapy; predominantly group Schema Therapy; optimal treatment as usual. Predominantly group treatment included some individual sessions, so it must not be called exclusively group therapy. The combined arm was not an individual-therapy-only arm.
- **Primary finding:** combined individual/group treatment improved BPD severity more than both usual treatment and predominantly group treatment. Predominantly group treatment did **not** significantly outperform usual treatment on that primary outcome. The guide does not need to reproduce numerical effect sizes or symptom cutoffs.
- **Study limitations:** attrition; deviations from planned site/cohort arrangements; inability to rate usual-treatment recordings for differentiation; therapists' limited prior experience with the group format; and unstandardized usual treatment. The trial does not establish superiority to a specified alternative specialist psychotherapy in a controlled head-to-head comparison.
- **Use boundary:** evidence for a complete professionally delivered treatment in the studied population. This was not a trial selecting people for Abandonment/Instability alone, not a test of causal schema mechanisms, not evidence for a single contact exercise, and not a test of this guide or its Persian translation. Recognizing an abandonment theme does not imply BPD.

## Editorial decisions and excluded inferences

- Preserve the three reading paths and all fifteen template headings. Needs are explained in section 3, separately from possible origins in section 5. References remain the last clinical content.
- Use the delayed nonurgent reply from a normally reliable friend as one repeated illustration. This is distinct from a missed urgent obligation or a chronically unreliable relationship. The reader should never be told that every concern is a false alarm.
- The overcompensation example concerns forceful control of availability; simply requesting comfort or sending another message is insufficient. Surrender is separately illustrated by treating persistent insecurity as inevitable. Actual constraints, fear, or limited options must be considered before assigning either label.
- Keep six specified modes and the shared glossary. Distinguish a possible angry protest from a controlling strategy and a punitive self-judgment. Do not imply the mode model is a personality-disorder diagnosis.
- Name nurturance and acceptance alongside stability. Distinguish a need for connection from the particular strategy of immediate access. Respect closeness, family culture, caregiving, disability-related support, and shared responsibilities.
- Do not require independence from necessary assistance. A practical backup plan may address a real support gap; such a gap is not evidence of incompetence.
- Keep optional reflection and practice original and unscored. A practical experiment is not an attachment test for someone else, an instruction to ignore broken commitments, or a substitute for support in danger or overwhelming distress.
- Describe therapist-supported imagery/dialogues using the reviewed sources. The approved plan allowed imagery or chair work; the selected verified material supports the imagery/dialogue option without adding an unreviewed procedural source.
- Further reading is clearly marked. No claim in the guide relies on unread *Reinventing Your Life* chapters. No questionnaire items, copyrighted inventories, diagnostic cutoffs, or test scoring are reproduced.
- Technical checks and content review establish implementation and editorial consistency only, not clinical effectiveness, psychometric validity, or a validated Persian adaptation.
