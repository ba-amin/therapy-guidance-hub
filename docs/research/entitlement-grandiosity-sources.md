The dialogue, bodily cues, scenarios, recognition prompts, and worksheets are original educational material. ## 1. Young, Klosko, and Weishaar — conceptual framework

<a id="ref-1"></a>
Young, J. E., Klosko, J. S., & Weishaar, M. E. (2003). *Schema Therapy: A Practitioner's Guide*. Guilford Press. [Official Chapter 1 excerpt](https://www.guilford.com/excerpts/young.pdf?t=1).

- **Access:** official excerpt, not the full book. Locators are printed pages; zero-based PDF indices are one less.
- **Passage locators:** pp. 9–11, five need groupings and possible developmental routes; pp. 15 and 18–19, Impaired Limits and Entitlement/Grandiosity; pp. 32–36, coping distinctions; Table 1.1, p. 39, Entitlement row; pp. 37–44, modes; pp. 44–47, assessment and treatment components.
- **Use:** definition, central needs, tentative origins, coping functions, collaborative therapy. Realistic limits/self-control is central to the conceptual formulation; attachment, expression, and autonomy may matter in an individual formulation.
- **Critical coping distinction:** Table 1.1 places imposing one's wishes/bragging under surrender, avoiding situations without superior status under avoidance, and excessive focus on others' needs under overcompensation. Thus assertive-looking behavior is not automatically overcompensation. Page 19 also describes entitlement as possible compensation for another schema, such as Emotional Deprivation.
- **Limits:** clinical theory does not establish a universal history or specific causal pathway. Do not import stigmatizing descriptions, infer that every reader was indulged, or generalize historical efficacy statements. The guide's ordinary-life examples are original applications rather than adaptations of published cases.

## 2. International Society of Schema Therapy — definition and domain

<a id="ref-2"></a>
International Society of Schema Therapy. (n.d.). *Schema Therapy Central Concepts*. [Official overview](https://schematherapysociety.org/Schema-Therapy).

- **Access/locators:** full relevant webpage sections; Impaired Limits, schema 10 (Entitlement/Grandiosity), schema 11 (Insufficient Self-Control/Self-Discipline); comparison definitions for Emotional Deprivation, Defectiveness/Shame, Approval-Seeking, Subjugation, and Self-Sacrifice as relevant.
- **Supported distinction:** the central issue is unequal privilege, superiority, or exemption from reciprocity, sometimes expressed through power/control. This differs from seeking approval, ordinary confidence, or the mere presence of support needs.
- **Use:** opening brief and domain link; comparison of possible meanings in the same observable situation. The domain groups concepts, not diagnoses.
- **Limits:** professional conceptual guidance, not a diagnostic test or observational study. Cultural roles, agreed responsibilities, accessibility adjustments, and actual unfairness require contextual examination. Those counterexamples are original teaching applications, not validation findings.

## 3. Young and First — mode terminology

<a id="ref-3"></a>
Young, J. E., & First, M. (2003). *Schema Mode Listing*. Schema Therapy Institute. [Official listing](https://www.schematherapy.com/id72.htm).

- **Access/locators:** full listing text, especially entries 1–3 and 5–10, including the 2003 copyright attribution. The web extractor initially returned only the heading; opening at line 3 exposed all entries.
- **Use:** names and distinctions for Vulnerable Child, Angry Child, Impulsive/Undisciplined Child, Compliant Surrenderer, Detached Protector, Overcompensator, Punitive Parent, Demanding Parent, and Healthy Adult, where selected in the guide. Use the hub's glossary; “Demanding Parent” shortens “Demanding or Critical Parent.”
- **Precision:** Angry Child concerns frustrated core needs; Impulsive/Undisciplined Child can concern immediate wants and difficulty accepting delay. Feeling frustrated does not itself decide between them. Overcompensator requires a compensatory function, not merely a forceful appearance. Compliant Surrenderer is interpersonal submission, not every instance of surrender to any schema.
- **Limits:** state descriptions are not identities, diagnoses, or a required roster. Original inner sentences and bodily cues are illustrative and nonspecific. No later subtype, such as Self-Aggrandizer, is attributed to this particular listing.

## 4. Arntz and Jacob — case conceptualization and limits of the model

<a id="ref-4"></a>
Arntz, A., & Jacob, G. (2013). *Schema Therapy in Practice: An Introductory Guide to the Schema Mode Approach*. Wiley-Blackwell. [Official publisher excerpt](https://catalogimages.wiley.com/images/db/pdf/9781119962861.excerpt.pdf).

- **Access:** publisher excerpt only, not the full treatment manual. Printed page numbers are two greater than zero-based PDF indices.
- **Passage locators:** pp. 3–5, clinical approach; pp. 18–19, Impaired Limits and Entitlement; pp. 26–28 and Table 1.2, need framework and its heuristic status; pp. 29–33, coping; pp. 34–39, introduction to the mode concept.
- **Use:** limits can be supportive; need fulfillment considers both the individual and other people. Developmental possibilities include learning from models and overpermissive or excessively restrictive environments, without declaring a personal cause.
- **Important qualification:** pp. 27–28 call the need/domain relationship a clinically developed heuristic, not an experimentally derived taxonomy; the specific limits-to-schema developmental proposition was untested in that account. Do not convert this historical qualification into a claim that no later research exists.
- **Limits:** conceptual teaching, not a trial of this guide. Keep imagery/chair work within the therapist-supported overview. Do not reproduce the book's fictional or clinical cases or use its shorthand descriptions as judgments about the reader.

## 5. Pauwels and colleagues — measurement and schema-specific association

<a id="ref-5"></a>
Pauwels, E., Dierckx, E., Smits, D., Janssen, R., & Claes, L. (2018). Validation of the Young Schema Questionnaire-Short Form in a Flemish community sample. *Psychologica Belgica, 58*(1), 34–50. [DOI: 10.5334/pb.406](https://doi.org/10.5334/pb.406). [Consulted full article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6194525/).

- **Access/locators:** full article; Methods 2.1–2.3, Results Tables 3–6, Discussion limitations.
- **Design/population:** cross-sectional self-report study of 672 Flemish community adults, sampled to reflect age, gender, and education distributions across ages 18–75. Short-form items were selected from administration of the longer questionnaire; 15- and 16-schema models were examined.
- **Measurement finding:** both tested factor structures were supported in this sample; Entitlement internal consistency was .81 (Table 5).
- **Observational finding:** Entitlement scores were associated with lower Agreeableness in an age/gender-adjusted, stepwise multischema regression (Table 6, standardized coefficient −.20). This is a conditional statistical association, not a behavioral prediction for an individual.
- **Limits:** self-report/common-method effects, one community/language context, model selection, and uncorrected acquiescence in the personality measure. No experimental manipulation, developmental cause, diagnostic cutoff, or treatment effect was established. These results do not validate this guide's original prompts or its Persian translation. Questionnaire content is not reproduced.

## 6. Bamelis and colleagues — whole-treatment evidence

<a id="ref-6"></a>
Bamelis, L. L. M., Evers, S. M. A. A., Spinhoven, P., & Arntz, A. (2014). Results of a multicenter randomized controlled trial of the clinical effectiveness of schema therapy for personality disorders. *American Journal of Psychiatry, 171*(3), 305–322. [DOI: 10.1176/appi.ajp.2013.12040518](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2013.12040518). [Consulted abstract and metadata](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24322378/).

- **Access:** abstract and bibliographic record only; full article not consulted.
- **Design/population:** single-blind randomized trial at 12 Dutch mental health institutes; 323 people with cluster C, paranoid, histrionic, or narcissistic personality disorders. Schema Therapy n = 147, usual care n = 135, clarification-oriented psychotherapy n = 41.
- **Finding:** 50-session Schema Therapy had greater diagnostic recovery three years after treatment started than both comparison conditions. Interview-based outcomes favored Schema Therapy; self-report outcomes did not show between-treatment differences.
- **Limits:** whole-treatment evidence in a specified clinical population; no Entitlement-specific effect, individual technique effect, self-help effect, or validation of this page. Do not characterize the pooled sample as an Entitlement or narcissistic-personality-only trial. Do not infer unreported diagnosis-specific estimates from the abstract.

## Editorial decisions and claim boundaries

- Keep Entitlement/Grandiosity distinct from psychiatric diagnosis. One schema or a forceful disagreement does not establish narcissistic personality disorder; the guide does not offer diagnostic criteria.
- Needs, preferences, access requirements, expertise, and responsibilities can justify different arrangements. Equality of regard does not require identical support or automatic compliance with unfair rules.
- Reciprocal responsibility and self-compassion coexist. Understanding the function of coercion does not excuse harm or require another person to accept repair immediately.
- The guide's three coping styles are classified by their function relative to a specified schema. Overcorrection into rigid self-erasure is not the goal; neither is assuming concealed shame beneath every entitled response.
- Empirical sections report what was actually measured. Personality association, questionnaire reliability, professional-treatment outcomes, and this page's usefulness are different claims.
