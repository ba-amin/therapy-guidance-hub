# Reusable schema guide template draft

Copy this outline when drafting a guide. Follow the [authoring standard](schema-guide-standard.md) for the common structure, bilingual text, exercises, evidence, and styles. Replace bracketed prompts before turning the draft into reader-facing content.

Record the English and Persian schema names, intended reader, source edition, planned filename, and review status in the authoring notes. In the finished page, show a clear title, a brief description, and an accurate content review date. Reading-time estimates should reflect the finished text.

**Required for every schema:** complete all parts of section 7. Carry its coping functions, mode activation cues, and repeated sequence into the cycle, examples, worksheets, practice, and progress review. Follow the standard's [coverage requirements and glossary](schema-guide-standard.md#required-coverage-of-coping-styles-and-modes). Use existing shared styles and interactions for the finished HTML.

## 2 Understanding this schema

[Give a concise, cited definition and explain unfamiliar terms. Add an original illustrative thought: “When [trigger], I tend to believe [meaning or rule].” Describe this as a possible internal message, not a diagnostic statement or a quotation from a source.]

## 3 Healthy needs and flexible alternatives

[Compare this schema's concern with a healthy response using a short table. Useful dimensions include choice, flexibility, response to setbacks, relationship effects, and personal cost. Choose a comparison appropriate to this schema.]

## 4 Recognizing the pattern

[Describe possible thoughts, feelings, body sensations, actions, and triggers. Identify early cues that can be revisited in section 7. Include a counterexample or contextual qualification so ordinary behavior is not automatically interpreted as a schema.]

## 5 Possible origins and unmet needs

[Present plausible developmental explanations using qualified language and citations. Identify relevant needs within a named framework. Allow for present pressures and different histories; do not instruct readers to recover a particular memory.]

## 6 How the cycle continues

[Use one explicitly fictional situation to fill six steps: trigger → meaning or rule → coping response → immediate effect → later cost → reinforcement. Within these steps, identify the possible activated mode, feelings/body cues, coping function, and any subsequent mode change. Show the conclusion that carries into the next similar situation. Explain what the response achieves or maintains in the short term; do not assume it always brings relief. Mark a Healthy Adult interruption and link to the expanded sequence in section 7.]

### 7.4 A recurring sequence and a different branch

**Fictional example / مثال فرضی**

[Expand the section 6 situation using these six steps. Supply the complete example in both languages. Keep one full version: use a compact cycle in section 6 and expand it here, or link here to a complete sequence already in section 6. Do not duplicate the full story.]

1. **Trigger:** [Observable event and context.]
2. **Meaning and state:** [Schema message, feelings/body, and a tentative mode name.]
3. **Coping response:** [Action or mental strategy; why surrender, avoidance, or overcompensation fits its function.]
4. **Immediate effect:** [Relief, familiar certainty, continued distress, or another plausible effect.]
5. **Later cost or shift:** [What happens next; how the person might move into another mode or coping response.]
6. **Repetition:** [Conclusion that reinforces the expectation and shapes the next similar situation.]

**A different branch / مسیر متفاوت:** [Return to the same trigger and show another coping style in two or three sentences. Explain its different function and possible consequence. This is an alternative possibility, not a required next stage.]

**Healthy Adult interruption / مداخلهٔ بزرگسال سالم:** [Point to a specific step. Add one original validating sentence, a manageable action, the need addressed, and a possible mixed outcome.]

[End with a brief invitation to recognize the sequence without forcing a match: “Does any part fit, does your sequence differ, or are you unsure?” / «آیا بخشی از این توالی با تجربهٔ شما جور است، توالی شما متفاوت است، یا مطمئن نیستید؟» Explain that the order can vary and states can overlap.]

## 8 Everyday examples

[Use several everyday contexts and one complete worked example. Connect situation, interpretation, possible mode, coping response and function, consequence, need, and flexible alternative. Reuse the section 7 example as the complete case if helpful; add another setting or coping response to show variation. Include a plausible mixed outcome rather than making every experiment succeed. Mark all invented scenarios as fictional.]

## 9 Related patterns

[Compare the central concern with nearby schemas. Avoid classifying a person from one behavior. Include a concise explanation that identifying with a schema does not establish a psychiatric diagnosis.]

## 10 Examining your experience

[Add a few original, optional recognition prompts. Do not assign points, severity bands, or a threshold. Use the standard eight-field reflection worksheet and a completed fictional example to show how it works.]

The worksheet covers situation, meaning, feelings, response and function, immediate and later consequences, needs, alternatives, and outcome or support. Keep one set of inputs shared between languages.

[Include the standard's bilingual helper prompts inside those eight fields: early internal message; state or mode and any shift; coping function; what repeated or changed; Healthy Adult alternative; cue or support for next time. Keep labels optional and allow “not sure / does not fit.” Show a completed fictional entry that follows section 7 without adding a separate mode test or duplicating inputs.]

## 11 Practicing change

[Offer a small choice the reader can adapt. Explain the purpose, appropriate context, and what to observe. Include a completed fictional experiment using the six fields: situation and limits, prediction, small action, support or stopping conditions, observed outcome, and learning.]

Sample learning prompt: “What did I observe, what remains uncertain, and what would I adjust next time?” Label the exercise as original and avoid promising that a different response will produce agreement, acceptance, or immediate relief.

[In the existing prediction field, name the state or coping urge the person expects. In the support field, plan one Healthy Adult response if it appears. In learning, notice whether the expected sequence repeated or changed. Do not make the absence of difficult modes a success condition.]

## 12 How therapy can help

[Describe how a therapist could assess the pattern, explore the function of coping, and clarify mode shifts collaboratively before selecting relevant approaches. Separate a general explanation of cognitive, behavioral, relational, or experiential work from instructions for independent practice. Keep intensive imagery or chair work in the therapist-supported overview. Cite the treatment sources used.]

## 13 Reviewing progress

[Suggest observable changes specific to this schema: noticing an activation cue, understanding a coping function, pausing during a mode shift, responding to a need, repairing harm, or recovering after a setback. Noticing afterward is useful too. Add a short closing reflection here. Let readers choose what matters to them without a compulsory streak, mode frequency score, or deadline for recovery.]

## 14 What research supports

[Separate conceptual sources, observational studies, treatment trials, and supplementary guidance. For each empirical finding, record population, design, comparison, outcome, and an important limitation. State whether it concerns the specific schema, a whole therapy, or a related construct.]

[If suitable direct evidence has not been verified, keep the claim narrow. Do not fill this section with generic efficacy claims. A draft should retain an evidence-check note until the cited source supports the intended wording.]
