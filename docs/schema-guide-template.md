# Reusable schema guide template draft

Copy this outline when drafting a guide. Follow the [authoring standard](schema-guide-standard.md) for the common structure, bilingual text, exercises, evidence, and styles. Replace bracketed prompts before turning the draft into reader-facing content.

Record the English and Persian schema names, intended reader, source edition, planned filename, and review status in the authoring notes. In the finished page, show a clear title, a brief description, and an accurate content review date. Reading-time estimates should reflect the finished text.

## 1 Getting started

[Explain what the reader can understand and explore. Add the three reading paths. State that recognition prompts support reflection and do not establish a diagnosis. Introduce optional exercises and briefly explain how answers are handled.]

Sample opening pattern: “This guide explores [pattern] and how it may affect [relevant experiences]. You can read the examples, examine a recent situation, or choose one small practice that fits your circumstances.”

## 2 Understanding this schema

[Give a concise, cited definition and explain unfamiliar terms. Add an original illustrative thought: “When [trigger], I tend to believe [meaning or rule].” Describe this as a possible internal message, not a diagnostic statement or a quotation from a source.]

## 3 Healthy needs and flexible alternatives

[Compare this schema's concern with a healthy response using a short table. Useful dimensions include choice, flexibility, response to setbacks, relationship effects, and personal cost. Choose a comparison appropriate to this schema.]

## 4 Recognizing the pattern

[Describe possible thoughts, feelings, body sensations, actions, and triggers. Include a counterexample or contextual qualification so ordinary behavior is not automatically interpreted as a schema.]

## 5 Possible origins and unmet needs

[Present plausible developmental explanations using qualified language and citations. Identify relevant needs within a named framework. Allow for present pressures and different histories; do not instruct readers to recover a particular memory.]

## 6 How the cycle continues

[Use one explicitly fictional situation to fill six steps: trigger → meaning or rule → coping response → immediate effect → later cost → reinforcement. Explain what the response achieves in the short term. Show one place a different choice might interrupt the sequence.]

## 7 Coping styles and modes

[Briefly distinguish coping styles from modes. For each coping example, identify the belief and function before naming the style. Explain only the modes useful for this guide, with cited definitions and original sample dialogue.]

## 8 Everyday examples

[Use several everyday contexts and one complete worked example. Each example should connect a situation, interpretation, response, consequence, need, and flexible alternative. Include a plausible mixed outcome rather than making every experiment succeed. Mark all invented scenarios as fictional.]

## 9 Related patterns

[Compare the central concern with nearby schemas. Avoid classifying a person from one behavior. Include a concise explanation that identifying with a schema does not establish a psychiatric diagnosis.]

## 10 Examining your experience

[Add a few original, optional recognition prompts. Do not assign points, severity bands, or a threshold. Use the standard eight-field reflection worksheet and a completed fictional example to show how it works.]

The worksheet covers situation, meaning, feelings, response and function, immediate and later consequences, needs, alternatives, and outcome or support. Keep one set of inputs shared between languages.

## 11 Practicing change

[Offer a small choice the reader can adapt. Explain the purpose, appropriate context, and what to observe. Include a completed fictional experiment using the six fields: situation and limits, prediction, small action, support or stopping conditions, observed outcome, and learning.]

Sample learning prompt: “What did I observe, what remains uncertain, and what would I adjust next time?” Label the exercise as original and avoid promising that a different response will produce agreement, acceptance, or immediate relief.

## 12 How therapy can help

[Describe how a therapist could assess the pattern and select relevant approaches. Separate a general explanation of cognitive, behavioral, relational, or experiential work from instructions for independent practice. Keep intensive imagery or chair work in the therapist-supported overview. Cite the treatment sources used.]

## 13 Reviewing progress

[Suggest observable changes specific to this schema, including flexibility and recovery after setbacks. Add a short closing reflection here. Let readers choose what matters to them without a compulsory streak, achievement score, or deadline for recovery.]

## 14 What research supports

[Separate conceptual sources, observational studies, treatment trials, and supplementary guidance. For each empirical finding, record population, design, comparison, outcome, and an important limitation. State whether it concerns the specific schema, a whole therapy, or a related construct.]

[If suitable direct evidence has not been verified, keep the claim narrow. Do not fill this section with generic efficacy claims. A draft should retain an evidence-check note until the cited source supports the intended wording.]

## 15 References and further reading

[Number every cited source and supply complete bibliographic details and verified links. Match citations across languages. Separate material actually consulted from recommended further reading, and identify an excerpt or abstract when that was the material available.]

Reference format: **[Number]. Author(s). (Year). Title. Journal and volume/pages, or publisher and edition. DOI or stable source link.**
